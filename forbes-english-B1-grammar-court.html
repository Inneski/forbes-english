Case 1 · Language focus

Present Perfect vs Past Simple

Does the sentence reach into now, or did it finish in the past? That single question decides the tense.

Present Perfect

A connection to now. I have lost my keys. — they are still lost.

Triggers: ever, never, just, already, yet, since, for

Past Simple

A finished moment in the past. I lost my keys yesterday.

Triggers: yesterday, last week, in 2019, ago, when

In Spanish: Present Perfect = pretérito perfecto («he perdido»); Past Simple = pretérito indefinido («perdí»). In German both often come out as the Perfekt, which is exactly why the distinction has to be learned rather than translated.

Case 2 · Language focus

The Second Conditional

For situations that are imaginary or unlikely right now — not for things that may well happen.

The formula

If + past simple, … would + infinitive

Either half can come first: I would travel if I had the money.

The trap

Never put would in the if-clause. If I would have money is wrong.

And use were for every person: If I were you…

This is the Spanish condicional simple: «Si tuviera dinero, viajaría.» The German equivalent is the Konjunktiv II: «Wenn ich Geld hätte, würde ich reisen.»

Case 3 · Language focus

Modals of Deduction

These modals do not talk about permission or ability. They say how sure you are, on the evidence in front of you.

must

Almost certain, positive.
She must be tired — she has been working all day.

can’t

Almost certain, negative.
That can’t be right — I just checked.

might / could

Possible, not certain.
He might be at the gym.

All three take the bare infinitive: must be, can’t know. For deduction about the past, add have + past participle: must have left, can’t have arrived.

Case 4 · Language focus

Reported Speech

When you report what someone said, every tense takes one step back into the past.

The shift

am → was
work → worked
will → would
have finished → had finished
can → could

say or tell

say takes no person: She said (that) she was tired.

tell needs one: He told me (that) he worked there.

Time words move too: tomorrow → the next day, yesterday → the day before, here → there.

Case 5 · Language focus

Relative Clauses

One pronoun joins two ideas into one sentence. Which pronoun depends on what you are pointing at.

People and things

who — people: the man who called
which — things: the book which I read
that — either, defining clauses only

Places and possession

where — places: the city where I grew up
whose — possession: the woman whose car was stolen

If the pronoun is the object of the clause you can usually drop it: the film (that) I watched. After a comma — a non-defining clause — that is never allowed.

Case 6 · Language focus

The Passive Voice

The passive moves the spotlight off whoever did it and onto what was done.

The formula

subject + be (conjugated) + past participle (+ by + agent)

Only be changes tense. The participle never moves.

Across the tenses

The reports are written every month.
The window was broken last night.
The project has been completed.
The form must be signed.

Drop by + agent when it is obvious, unknown, or beside the point — which is most of the time.

Case 7 · Language focus

Linking & Time Connectives

Most connective errors are grammar errors, not meaning errors: what follows the word is what decides it.

Contrast

although / even though + clause (subject + verb)
despite / in spite of + noun or -ing

Although it rained… / Despite the rain…

Time and condition

as soon as — immediately after
by the time — before a moment
unless — except if
provided that — on condition that

unless already contains the negative, so the verb after it stays positive: Unless you leave now… means If you don’t leave now…
