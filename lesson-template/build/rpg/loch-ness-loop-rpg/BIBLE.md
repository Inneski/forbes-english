# THE LOCH NESS LOOP — story bible (binding for every writing agent)

A Block Camp RPG about hiring a bike in Inverness and riding the loop round
Loch Ness: the bike shop, the road, a pub in Fort Augustus, an old man's
boat at dawn, and the monster. Written to `docs/CHATGPT-RPG-BRIEF.md` and
`lesson-template/build/rpg/README.md`, in the shape of
`rpg/kraken-black-tide-rpg/` (script.json → assemble-script.py → data.json)
and the spirit of `rpg/kraken-saga/STORY.md` (half the questions are
concept checks on what somebody just said).

**Unlike the saga, every panel in this script is inside the engine's word
budgets**, so it can be built the day the plates arrive. That is the point of
this file: a long panel is not a rich scene, it is a broken one.

## Two kinds of question

- **[VOCAB]** — a gap in a line of dialogue, or a "which is it?" from a
  character, where the answer is a bike part or a bike verb. The two wrong
  options are real B2 learner errors (see "Writing a VOCAB item"). Nine of
  the eighteen slots.
- **[CCQ]** — a concept-checking question. Somebody tells you something
  (the hire terms, which road to take, where and when to look, what to do if
  she comes up) and then a character asks you what was said or what it
  means. You answer from the words on the panel, never from knowledge of
  Loch Ness, never from grammar. Nine of the eighteen slots.

Both kinds are scored the same, and a player meets both kinds on every path.

## Cast — use these names and nothing else

- **Alex Marin** — you. **A Swiss film director from Zürich**, here between
  projects (Innes, 2026-09-17). Edit suites, not saddles: has not been on a
  bike since film school. Has come to ride the loop and, privately, to see
  her — there may be a film in it, which is never said aloud on a panel but
  is why Alex carries a camera and why Moira asks "Are we in it?". The
  country is said once, at `pub1`, after Moira has guessed it wrong twice;
  nowhere on any panel is Alex from "a flat city" or from anywhere but
  Switzerland.
- **Shona Nicolson** — runs the bike shop by the river in Inverness. Tall,
  early thirties, long copper-red hair, freckles. Brisk, dry, exact with
  numbers, and everything she says on Wednesday morning turns out to matter
  later. She takes a photo of everyone who hires, for the wall of the shop.
- **Moira Fraser** — sixties, runs a B&B in Fort Augustus, big laugh, a pint.
  Has seen her twice. Guesses where you are from, wrongly, twice.
- **Jean Cameron** — fifties, a half of lager, quiet and exact. Twenty years
  with a cycling club and does not believe in the monster. Hears your brakes
  rubbing from the bar and fixes them with a multi-tool. Sends you to Archie.
- **Archie MacRae** — seventies, fifty years on the loch, an old wooden
  launch with a cabin and an inboard engine. Takes people out for the price
  of the diesel. Says less than he knows. Has rules aboard.
- **Her** — the locals never say "monster" or "Nessie"; they say *her*,
  *herself*, *the big one*. Seen once, at fifty yards, at first light: a long
  wet back, a wake with nothing in front of it, a neck that comes up and
  goes down. Never a full body.

## Places — real, and named as the locals name them

- **Inverness** — the shop is by the river, ten minutes from the station.
- **Dores** — the beach at the top of the loch where the road forks:
  the **shore road** along the water, or the **high road** over the hills.
- **The shore road** — by the loch, past **Foyers** (the waterfall, the café,
  and the steep climb out of the village).
- **The high road** — up past the small lochs and over the moor by
  **Whitebridge**: sheep, cattle grids, one farm, no shop, a long descent.
- **Fort Augustus** — the village at the foot of the loch: the canal locks,
  the pier, the pub by the locks, Moira's B&B.
- **The castle** — Urquhart Castle, halfway up the north side. The deep
  water off it is where the sonar boats go.
- **The bay** — where the river comes into the loch at Fort Augustus.
  Shallow, weedy, birds. Where the old folk saw her.
- **The north side** — the A82, busy, lorries, no verge; and the Great Glen
  Way, the walkers' path above it. Archie puts you off at the castle so you
  ride only the last stretch.

## Tone

A holiday that turns into a story. Warm, dry, Scottish, funny in the
dialogue, never sneering at the visitor. The monster is treated straight:
nobody on the panel laughs at her, and the sighting is quiet, not a jump
scare. Alex is a competent adult — a director who frames every view without
meaning to — who has never heard the words for a bike in English; that is
the whole joke and the whole lesson. The film is a reason to carry a camera
and to be teased, never a plot.

## Hard rules for every line you write

1. **Word budgets are walls, not aims.** The panel does not scroll.
   - `title` 3–4 words, **max 5**
   - `act` 4 words, **max 6**
   - `story` 7–10 words, **max 28** — the speech goes here
   - `clue` 10 words, **max 16**
   - `prompt` 7 words, **max 13**
   - each option 25 characters, **max 45**
   - `explanation` 12 words, **max 25**
   - cover `lead` **max 35**; briefing card `text` **max 12**; route `note` **max 14**
2. **Every scene stands alone.** The player reads one panel and the last one
   is gone. Never "as she said", never "remember the".
3. **Straight apostrophes and quotes only.** No curly quotes anywhere.
4. Titles in CAPITALS. The player is "you". The Scots in the dialogue is
   light — *aye*, *wee*, *the back of six*, *no bother*, *I'd not* — at most
   one such idiom per scene, and always inferable from the panel.
5. **Never mention a previous version of anything**, and never a real
   business, boat, café or person by name. The shop is Shona's shop; the
   pub is the pub by the locks.
6. The explanation **names the reason**, it does not repeat the answer.
7. Answer options are English and are never translated or glossed.
8. **No lettering may be needed in any picture.** A clue names a physical
   object — the tyre lever, the battery, the pint glass — never a sign, a
   price card, a map, a phone screen with words on it or a hire form.
   ("the card by the till" is fine as a *phrase* in the speech; it must not
   be what the clue asks the player to look at.)
9. **Nothing is said twice.** Two branches run in parallel and a player who
   replays takes the other one. No sentence, image, joke or reveal may
   appear on both the shore road and the high road, or on both the castle
   and the bay. The briefing must not print a string that is also a correct
   option.
10. **Both branches of a fork carry the same slots in the same order**, so
    every player meets all eighteen points and both paths score out of 90.

## The CAPS convention — hardwired, and not in HOUSE-STYLE.md

This rule lives in the shipped builders and every draft that has not been
told it has broken it. Read it twice.

**In a VOCAB explanation the target words go in CAPS.** The key, and any
other bike part you name to rule a distractor out. Never lowercase prose,
never a description in place of the word:

    explanation  The SEAT POST is the tube the saddle sits on; it slides
                 into the frame. The HEAD TUBE holds the fork.

    explanation  The chain COMES OFF the chainring. "Fallen" and "gone out"
                 are translations, not English; a chain does not fall.

**In a CCQ explanation the decisive words of the speech go in double
quotes** — you are citing what was said, so cite it:

    explanation  Shona said "back by six or it's a third day". Half past six
                 is after six, so the third day is charged.

**Briefing cards** put the part names in CAPS, as a list:

    head   THE WHEEL
    text   TYRE · INNER TUBE · VALVE · RIM · SPOKES · HUB

A seam — one chapter in CAPS and the next in lowercase — is a blocker.
check-items.py refuses a VOCAB explanation with no CAPS token and a CCQ
explanation with no quoted citation.

## Writing a VOCAB item

- **The key is the only option a British bike mechanic would accept.** A
  distractor that a teacher would have to mark correct is the worst defect
  this repo knows, and the everyday bike words are full of synonyms. **Never
  use these as distractors**, because they are acceptable English: *seat*
  for saddle, *flat* or *flat tyre* for puncture, *bars* for handlebars,
  *shift down* for change down, *cogs* or *sprockets* for the cassette,
  *dropped the chain* / *the chain jumped off* for the chain came off,
  *bent* for a buckled wheel, *lamp* for a light, *sound* for ring (a bell),
  *crossbar* for top tube, *gear lever* for gear shifter.
- Each distractor belongs to one of three **families** (declared in
  `families.json`, two per scene):
  - **PART** — another real bike part, ruled out by the clue's description
    of position or function ("the tube under the saddle" is not the head
    tube).
  - **TRANSFER** — a word-for-word translation from the learner's language
    that is not English on a bike: *the steering*, *the handles*, *the chain
    has fallen*, *the tyre is empty*, *quick lock*, *gear changer*.
  - **COLLOC** — the right noun with the wrong verb or preposition: *pump
    the tyre until sixty*, *change lower before the hill*, *lock it at the
    railing*.
  No family may carry more than 55% of the items on any path, and no two
  consecutive items on a path may share a family pair.
- **The clue describes the part by position and function and never names
  it.** If the key word appears anywhere in the story, clue or prompt, the
  question is free — check-items.py refuses it.
- **Write all three options within two characters of each other.** The key
  must never be the longest, and a key that is always the shortest is the
  same free elimination the other way. Ties are the target.
- **Kill the blend tell.** In a three-option minimal pair the key is usually
  the intersection of its distractors' good halves. The key must own at
  least one word that appears in neither distractor.
- No option is nonsense, a joke, or a spelling trap.

## Writing a CCQ item

- **The speech is on the panel**, in the story field, quoted, inside 28
  words including any narration. The clue adds one fact or the setting. The
  prompt asks one thing about what was said or what it means. The answer is
  decidable from the panel alone; a player who has never heard of Loch Ness
  can get it.
- The question is about **meaning, not memory**: what will happen, what she
  is recommending, which two things they agree on, what he is telling you
  not to do, when exactly. Implicature is fair game at B2 — *"I'd not take
  the e-bike if I were you"* is a recommendation of the other bike.
- Each distractor belongs to one of four **families**:
  - **NUMBER** — a figure that appears in the speech, attached to the wrong
    thing (the price, not the deposit; the boats, not the children).
  - **PARTIAL** — something the speaker did say, but not the answer to this
    question.
  - **OPPOSITE** — the reverse of what was said, or an implicature the
    speaker cancelled.
  - **INFER** — a plausible inference the speaker did not make.
- **The key must not appear verbatim in the story or clue.** A CCQ whose
  answer is a copy of a phrase on the panel is a reading test, not a
  concept check.
- Options within two characters of each other; the key never the longest.

## Endings — the beats

- `master` — every answer right, four photos. Shona zooms in on the third
  photo, says "That's her", and pins it on the wall next to the one of you.
  Train at eight.
- `complete` — passed. The third photo is a wake and a rain-spotted lens.
  Shona: "Aye, that's the loch." Eighty-five pounds back and sore legs.
- `missing` — finished, short of photos. The loop is done; the phone was in
  the bag when it mattered. Shona hands the deposit back and asks what you
  saw. You tell her. She has heard it before.
- `failed` — out of chances. The bike comes home on the bus from Fort
  Augustus with you beside it. Shona counts the kit and does not ask. The
  loch keeps herself to herself.

## The briefing — five cards and a note

The rules screen is the vocabulary reference the player can consult. Five
cards, each a heading and a list of parts in CAPS separated by ` · `, twelve
words maximum; and one note. The cards must not print a string that is also
a correct option. Suggested shape (the writer may improve the wording, not
the coverage):

1. THE WHEEL — TYRE · INNER TUBE · VALVE · RIM · SPOKES · HUB
2. THE DRIVE — CHAIN · CHAINRING · CASSETTE · DERAILLEUR · CRANK · PEDALS
3. THE CONTROLS — HANDLEBARS · BRAKE LEVERS · BRAKE PADS · GEAR SHIFTERS
4. THE FRAME AND THE REST — TOP TUBE · SEAT POST · SADDLE · MUDGUARD · PANNIER RACK
5. THE VERBS — PUMP UP · CHANGE DOWN · COME OFF · RUB · SLIP · FREEWHEEL

Note: on a British bike the RIGHT lever is the FRONT brake.

## Route choices

Two, `dores` and `pier`. Each has an act kicker, a title, a story (max 28
words), and two routes with a `route` name in CAPS (SHORE ROAD / HIGH ROAD;
THE CASTLE / THE BAY), a `label` button (max 5 words) and a `note` (max 14
words). Both notes must be honest about what the branch is like, and neither
may promise a beat the branch does not deliver.
