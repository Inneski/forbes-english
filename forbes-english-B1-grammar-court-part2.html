Case 1 · Language focus

Used to & Would

Both describe the past that is no longer true. Only one of them can describe a state.

Repeated actions

Both work. I used to walk to school. / I would walk to school.

States

Only used to. I used to be shy. — never I would be shy.

State verbs: be, have, know, like, believe

Questions and negatives drop the -d: Did you use to…? and I didn’t use to… — would is not used this way at all.

Case 2 · Language focus

The Third Conditional

An imagined past. Not a plan, not a possibility — a version of events that did not happen and now cannot.

The formula

If + past perfect, … would have + past participle

If I had studied, I would have passed.

Second or third?

Second conditional = an imaginary present.
Third conditional = an imaginary past.

If the weather were better… vs If the weather had been better…

Both halves have to be in their own form. Mixing would into the if-clause, or a plain past simple into the main clause, is the usual failure.

Case 3 · Language focus

Gerunds & Infinitives

There is no rule you can reason your way to. The verb in front decides, and the lists have to be learned.

+ gerund (-ing)

enjoy, avoid, finish, suggest, mind, consider, keep, miss, practise, deny, admit

+ infinitive (to…)

want, need, decide, hope, plan, afford, agree, offer, refuse, manage, seem, appear

+ both, meaning changes

remember, forget, stop, try, go on, regret

He stopped smoking ≠ He stopped to smoke

After a preposition it is always -ing: good at cooking, instead of going. That one you can rely on.

Case 4 · Language focus

Quantifiers

Two questions decide every one of these: can you count the noun, and is the amount a problem or a comfort?

Too much / too many

too many + countable plural · too much + uncountable

Both mean more than needed — always a problem.

A few / a little

a few + countable · a little + uncountable — both positive.

Drop the a and the meaning flips: few / little mean not many, not much.

enough goes before a noun but after an adjective: enough time, old enough. plenty of takes either kind of noun and is always positive.

Case 5 · Language focus

Indirect Questions

A question wrapped in a polite opening stops behaving like a question. The word order goes back to a statement.

The openings

Could you tell me + wh-word + subject + verb
Do you know + if / whether + subject + verb
I was wondering / Would you mind telling me

Two things vanish

No do / does / did, and no inversion.

Where is the station? → …where the station is.
Does she work here? → …if she works here.

The question mark stays, because the whole sentence is still a question. Only the inner clause becomes a statement.

Case 6 · Language focus

Perfect Simple vs Continuous

Same stretch of time, different question. Are you counting what got finished, or describing how long it went on?

Simple — the result

I’ve written three reports. Finished, and the number matters.

Triggers: already, just, yet, three times, how many

Continuous — the process

I’ve been writing all morning. The duration is the point, and it often shows.

Triggers: for, since, all day, how long, lately

A countable quantity almost never takes the continuous: how many emails have you sent, not have you been sending.

Case 7 · Language focus

Wishes & Regrets

After wish and if only, the tense goes one step further back than the time you are talking about.

About now

wish / if only + past simple
I wish I knew the answer.

For be, the exam form is were: I wish I were taller.

About the past

wish / if only + past perfect
I wish I had studied harder.

This is regret. Nothing can be done about it now.

There is a third pattern for somebody else’s annoying habit: wish + would + infinitive — I wish you would stop that.
