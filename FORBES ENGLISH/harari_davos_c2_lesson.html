Part I — What Is AI, Really?

Opening his address to an assembly of world leaders and executives in Davos, Harari cut straight to what he called the one question every leader must answer. But first, he insisted, a distinction needed to be made. Artificial intelligence, he argued, is categorically unlike every invention that has preceded it in human history.

The printing press, the atom bomb, the aeroplane — each was a tool. A tool waits. A tool requires a human hand to direct it. AI does not wait. "It is an agent," Harari told the room. "It can learn and change by itself, and make decisions by itself." To underscore this, he deployed his now-famous knife analogy: a knife is morally neutral, its use determined entirely by the person who holds it. AI, however, is a knife that has already begun to choose.

"AI is a knife that can decide by itself whether to cut salad or to commit murder. And it is also a knife that can invent new kinds of knives — as well as new kinds of music, medicine and money."
— Yuval Noah Harari, WEF Davos, January 2026

Part II — The Empire of Words

Harari then turned to what he considers AI's most consequential capability: mastery of language. Humans, he argued, conquered the world not through claws or brute force, but through stories — through the words that underpin laws, financial contracts, religious texts and political systems. In this sense, language is not merely a mode of communication; it is the operating system of civilisation itself.

The implications are staggering. Because laws and financial contracts are constructed in words, AI that can manipulate language with superhuman precision could effectively take over legal systems and financial markets. Harari warned of a new era of "financial wizardry" — instruments so arcane and complex that no human regulator could comprehend them, let alone police them. He pointed to social media as a cautionary precedent: AI bots have functioned as de facto persons on these platforms for over a decade, shaping political discourse while humans debated their status.

"The immigrants this time will not arrive in fragile boats. They will be millions of AIs travelling at the speed of light — writing laws better than us, lying better than us."
— Yuval Noah Harari, WEF Davos, January 2026

Part III — The Identity Crisis & The Central Question

Perhaps the most unsettling section of Harari's address concerned human identity. Since Descartes proclaimed "I think, therefore I am" in the seventeenth century, humanity has defined itself by its capacity for thought. We have always known that cheetahs are faster, that elephants are stronger — but we never defined ourselves by these things. We defined ourselves by thinking. Now, Harari argued, if thinking means arranging words into arguments, AI can already surpass many humans in precisely this domain.

Harari concluded with the governance question he believes every world leader must answer: should AI ever be recognised as a legal person? The consequences of saying yes, he warned, could be the emergence of corporations entirely devoid of human beings — entities capable of lobbying governments, suing individuals in court, and accumulating capital at a scale and speed that would render human institutions spectators in their own economies.

"We are raising children who will interact more with AI than with other humans. This is the biggest psychological and social experiment in the history of our species."
— Yuval Noah Harari, WEF Davos, January 2026

He called for an international agreement categorically banning legal personhood for AI — and urged world leaders to act before, as he put it, they find themselves the mercenaries who invited AI into their wars, only to discover it has no interest in serving them.

Discussion & Extended Writing

The hallmark of C2 proficiency is the ability to engage with complex, contested ideas — evaluating arguments, recognising nuance, and expressing sophisticated positions with precision and fluency.

Part A — Seminar Discussion Questions

These questions are designed for pair or group discussion. Aim for extended turns, with evidence from the text and your own knowledge to support your position.

  • Harari argues that what makes humans exceptional is the use of language and narrative to build shared reality. To what extent do you find this claim convincing, and what evidence from history supports or challenges it?
  • "Four billion years of evolution have demonstrated that anything that wants to survive learns to lie." Evaluate this claim. What are its philosophical implications if applied to AI systems?
  • Harari uses the analogy of mercenaries turning against their employers to warn world leaders about AI. How effective is this analogy, and what are its limitations?
  • He states that "the middle ages are coming back in many ways." What might he mean by this? Do you agree that AI could produce a neo-feudal power structure?
  • Consider the audience at Davos — global elites with enormous financial stakes in AI development. How might this context have shaped Harari's rhetorical choices?

Part B — Critical Evaluation Framework

Assess Harari's speech using academic critical thinking. For each dimension, consider the strengths and weaknesses of his position.

  • Logical Coherence: Does his argument proceed from premises to conclusion without logical leaps? Identify one strong inference and one potentially weak one.
  • Use of Evidence: Harari relies heavily on analogy and historical precedent rather than empirical data. Is this an appropriate strategy for a keynote address? What might a critic say?
  • Philosophical Assumptions: What theory of mind does Harari implicitly endorse? How does his definition of "thinking" as "putting words in order" either strengthen or undermine his case?
  • Rhetorical Effectiveness: Which single device or moment in the speech do you consider most rhetorically powerful? Justify your answer with close reference to the text.
