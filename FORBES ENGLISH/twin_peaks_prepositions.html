Advanced Theory

Prepositional phrases: form and function

At the advanced level, a preposition is not just a small connector word. It is the hinge between a noun phrase and the role it plays in the sentence — as an adverbial (modifying a verb), an adjectival (modifying a noun), or as a nominal (acting as a noun itself).

Structure of a prepositional phrase
prepositionin
+
noun phrase (object)the Black Lodge
→
prepositional phrase
The preposition's object is ALWAYS a noun or pronoun — never a verb.

The three grammatical functions of a prepositional phrase

1 · Adverbial
Modifies a verb, adjective, or whole sentence. Answers: where? when? how? why?
Agent Cooper sat
in the diner.
She disappeared at dawn.
2 · Adjectival
Modifies a noun — answers: which one? what kind? Often called a post-modifier.
The woman
in the red room
spoke backwards.
3 · Nominal
Acts as a noun — as subject or object. Rare but important for formal writing.
Between the trees
is where he vanished.
(subject of "is")
Stranded prep.
A preposition can appear at the end of a clause in relative clauses and questions — called stranding.
The lodge he came from.
Who did she talk to?
🦉
The Log Lady warns The old rule "never end a sentence with a preposition" is a myth. Stranded prepositions are grammatically correct in English and completely natural. Only avoid them in very formal academic writing where the lodge from which he came would be preferred.

Prepositional phrases as post-modifiers

In noun phrases, a prepositional phrase almost always follows the noun it modifies. This is called a post-modifying prepositional phrase. It distinguishes one thing from another.

The owls [in the forest] are not what they seem. ← post-modifier: which owls?
A woman [with plastic wrapped around her] was found on the shore. ← post-modifier: which woman?
The man [from another place] moved like smoke. ← post-modifier: which man?

Prepositional phrases as adverbials: six sub-types

Adverbial typeQuestion it answersExample
PlaceWhere?Cooper sat at the Double R.
TimeWhen? How long?She vanished before sunrise.
DirectionWhere to/from?He walked into the Red Room.
MannerHow?He spoke in a low whisper.
Reason / PurposeWhy? For what?She went back for the ring.
ConcessionDespite what?Despite the danger, he entered.

Cooper's Dictaphone note due to vs. because of: Both show cause, but due to is more formal and traditionally follows a form of to be ("The delay was due to fog"). In modern English they are often interchangeable, but avoid starting a sentence with Due to… in academic writing — use Because of… instead.

Complex prepositions with "of"

Many complex prepositions follow the pattern preposition + noun + "of". These are formal and add precision:

In the absence of evidence, the case was closed. ← formal alternative to "without"
At the heart of every mystery is a question no one dares ask. ← formal spatial/figurative
By virtue of his badge, Cooper was allowed through. ← reason/justification
On the basis of the evidence, she was the prime suspect. ← formal reasoning
In the wake of the tragedy, the town fell silent. ← time: following an event

🌲
Tricky pair result in vs result from: The event results in the consequence. The consequence results from the event. "The investigation resulted in an arrest" (cause → effect). "The arrest resulted from the investigation" (effect ← cause).

The Double Agents

Preposition or conjunction?

Several words operate as both prepositions and conjunctions, depending on what follows them. This is one of the most tested distinctions at the advanced level. The rule is simple but easy to forget under pressure.

🪵
The key rule Preposition → followed by a noun / noun phrase (no subject + verb).
Conjunction → followed by a clause (subject + verb).
WordAs a preposition (+noun)As a conjunction (+clause)
before She left before dawn.
(before + noun)		 She left before the owl appeared.
(before + subject + verb)
after He arrived after midnight. He arrived after the lights went out.
since She'd been silent since Tuesday. She'd been silent since she saw the Red Room.
until They waited until nightfall. They waited until the fire truck appeared.
despite Despite the danger, he went in. Despite cannot be used as a conjunction — use although / even though + clause instead.
except Everyone stayed, except Cooper. Everyone stayed, except that Cooper left a note.
(needs "that" to be a conjunction)

The tricky "since" — time vs reason

Since is especially complex: it can be a preposition (time), a conjunction of time, or a conjunction of reason (meaning because). Context — and sometimes ambiguity — decides.

Since last winter, the diner had been quieter. ← preposition: time (from a point until now)
Since Laura died, the town has never been the same. ← conjunction of time
Since you know the truth, you must also know the danger. ← conjunction of reason (= because)

Preposition vs particle: phrasal verbs

Many prepositions double as verb particles in phrasal verbs. As a particle, the word is part of the verb — it changes the meaning completely and cannot be moved freely. As a preposition, it can be questioned with "where?" or moved to the end of a clause.

She looked up the case file. ← particle: "look up" = research. Cannot ask "Up where?"
She looked up the hill. ← preposition: "up" = direction. Can ask "Up where?"
He turned in the killer. ← particle: "turn in" = hand over to authorities
He turned in the doorway. ← preposition: location of turning
