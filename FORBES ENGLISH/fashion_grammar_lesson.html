An introduction to active & passive voice

Language has a voice — and so does fashion. Today we explore active and passive voice through two legendary designers whose work speaks volumes: the theatrical fury of Alexander McQueen and the contemplative precision of Yohji Yamamoto.

1969 – 2010 · London

Known for dramatically theatrical runway shows, McQueen transformed fashion into performance art. His work was defined by darkness, romance, and technical mastery.

Yamamoto's deconstructed aesthetic challenged Western beauty ideals. His philosophy of clothing as poetry shaped avant-garde fashion for decades.

The Active Voice
Theory — Part One

In an active sentence, the subject performs the action. The subject is the doer — powerful, direct, and present. Like McQueen storming onto the runway.

Formula

[Subject / Doer] + [Verb] + [Object]

When to use active voice

Active voice is preferred when:

  • ◆  The doer is important and should be highlighted
  • ◆  You want a direct, energetic, clear sentence
  • ◆  Writing journalism, criticism, or personal statements
  • ◆  Academic writing and most everyday communication
The Passive Voice
Theory — Part Two

In a passive sentence, the object of the action becomes the grammatical subject. The doer may be mentioned — or may disappear entirely, like a silhouette in Yamamoto's monochromatic world.

Formula

[Object → new Subject] + [to be + past participle] + [by + agent (optional)]

The verb always requires to be in the correct tense + the past participle of the main verb.

When to use passive voice

  • ◆  When the doer is unknown or unimportant
  • ◆  To emphasise the action or the object receiving it
  • ◆  In scientific or formal writing ("The sample was analysed")
  • ◆  When you wish to be diplomatic — avoiding blame or praise
