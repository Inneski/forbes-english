Before you begin

The hide

a noun, not a verb

A hide is a small screened shelter that birdwatchers sit inside so they can watch without being seen — often a timber box on stilts at the edge of the water, with shutters to look through. Later on you will read about moving the hide further from the nest. That is this building being moved, not anyone concealing anything.

Before you begin

Four words the briefings assume you know

the hide

a small screened shelter that birdwatchers sit inside so they can watch without being seen — a noun here, never the verb

a cull

the licensed killing of part of a wild population, usually to control disease or numbers

a byelaw

a local rule made by a council or other public body and enforceable inside its own area

a sand martin

a small brown-and-white migrant that digs nest tunnels into soft vertical sand faces, often in worked-out pits

All four appear inside question stems later on. They are glossed here so that no item is testing whether you happen to know the jargon.

Confusable pairs

Three questions that separate a pair

1 · What does it go with?

You revoke a permit. You resist a temptation. Neither verb will take the other’s object.

2 · What follows it?

defraud sb of sth · swindle sb out of sth

3 · Who says it?

stressy, a gig, a grumble — spoken. anxious, a performance, a concern — written.

Every pair ahead yields to one of these three. Ask them in order: what it goes with, what follows it, who says it.

Confusable pairs

stressy / stressful

stressy

informal, and about a person’s manner: don’t be so stressy about it

stressful

about the situation that causes it: the most stressful week of her career

This pair turns on register as much as sense. Stressy belongs in the staff room; a written report says anxious.

Confusable pairs

physician / physicist

-ian → the practitioner

physician · optician · technician

-ist → the specialist, -er → the doer

physicist · biologist  |  programmer · offender · assessor

All three endings name a person, so the ending alone will not choose for you — but it does tell you the option is a person and not an act. A physician treats you; a physicist models your floodwater.

Confusable pairs

offence / offender

the act

a criminal offence · to commit an offence · an offence under the Act

the person

a repeat offender · first-time offenders · offenders face steeper fines

British English spells the act offence; American English spells it offense. The person is offender on both sides of the Atlantic. This lesson is British throughout.

Confusable pairs

mystical / mysterious

mystical

spiritual, sacred, beyond ordinary explanation: a mystical quality, mystical traditions

mysterious

simply hard to explain: a mysterious disappearance, a mysterious phone call

A quick test. If somebody has opened an inquiry, the thing is mysterious. If somebody has lit a candle, it is mystical.

Confusable pairs

defraud / swindle — what follows

defraud sb (of sth)

They tried to defraud the agency. · He defrauded her of £40,000.

swindle sb out of sth

He tried to swindle the landowner out of his grant. Never swindle him of.

Same crime, different frame. Out of is the single most reliable signal that swindle is the verb wanted: it is the pattern that decides, not the meaning.

Confusable pairs

One root, four slots

verb

defraud — to defraud the agency

noun

fraud — a fraud investigation; fraudster — the person

adjective

fraudulent — a fraudulent claim, fraudulently obtained

The verb, the noun and the adjective are each tested in a different section of this lesson. Knowing the family lets you move between them inside a sentence instead of reaching for the one form you remember.

Confusable pairs

legal / logical

legal

to do with the law: legal advice, legal confirmation, a legal requirement

logical

to do with reasoning: a logical argument, the logical next step

A decision can be legal and not logical, or logical and not legal. Ask which authority the sentence is appealing to: a statute, or an argument.

Confusable pairs

a requirement / several requirements

the determiner decides

a formal requirement · several strict requirements

and a collective noun takes either

the survey team prefer early starts · the team is small

British English lets a collective noun take a plural verb when you mean the individuals in it — the team prefer, the panel disagree, the government are divided. Both forms are correct; the meaning shifts.

Confusable pairs

revoke / resist

revoke + something you granted

a permit · a licence · consent · an offer · a decision

resist + something pulling at you

a temptation · pressure · the urge to help · arrest · change

Two verbs of refusal separated entirely by what follows them. Nothing grants you a temptation, and no permit pulls.

Field language

Report English and staff-room English

in the staff room

stressy · a gig · gossip · grumble · a people person · slick

in the written report

anxious · a performance · unconfirmed reports · raise concerns · works well with the public · efficient

Nothing in the left-hand column is wrong English. All of it is wrong in a document a landowner’s solicitor may read. Knowing a word is not the same as knowing where it goes.

Field language

Four teeth, front to back

incisor

flat and chisel-edged, at the front — for cutting

canine

long and pointed, beside the incisors — for gripping and tearing

premolar

behind the canine — for holding and shearing

molar

broad, at the back — for grinding

Carnivores carry the enlarged canine, which is the tooth the trapped fox had fractured. Three of these four turn up as wrong options and are worth knowing on sight.

Field language

crane, flock, survey

crane

first the bird: Grus grus, tall, grey and trumpeting. Then, off the reserve, the lifting machine.

flock

a group of birds of one kind, feeding or travelling together: a flock of forty cranes

survey

to examine and record an area: a habitat survey, the site survey noted several features

Two of these have a second meaning that arrives first for most learners — the machine, and land measurement. On a reserve it is the bird and the habitat sense that are in use.

Field language

Taking it into account

four ways to say it

take sth into account · take sth into consideration · bear sth in mind · factor sth in

spark and ignite

spark (n) a fiery particle; (v) to trigger: the disappearance sparked an inquiry. ignite: to catch fire, or to set alight.

The four phrases on the left are interchangeable in a briefing. Spark is the one that has quietly become a verb about causes rather than about fire — and that is now its commonest use in a report.

🗣Discussion · in pairs

A landowner has just been told his access permit is being revoked after a third breach.

  • One of you is the field officer. Deliver the decision, name the byelaw and hold the line when he pushes back.
  • Swap roles. This time the landowner says a consultant swindled him out of the grant that paid for the work. Take that into account — without reversing the decision.
  • Both: your draft newsletter says the team were stressy about the inspection and that Tomas had a gig. Argue the register, item by item, and agree a written version.
✍️Writing · 150–250 words

Write the site note that goes to the landowner’s solicitor: what the breach was, which byelaw applies, and what happens next. Report register throughout — nothing from the staff room.
