The Writer's Nightmare

Creativity, the block and the blank page

The Story

Part 1 · read
The story

The blank page

It is true — writer's block is a nightmare. Maya stares at the blank page every morning. She doesn't write a single word for days. The cursor blinks, consistent and merciless.

Every word in bold is taught in Part 2.

The story

The opposite effect

Forcing ideas has the opposite effect: the harder she tries, the less creative she feels. Some authors say it is exhausting to fight the block. Experts say that changes to a writer's routine need to be gradual.

The story

A different process

Her process is different from that of the other writers she knows. A fixed mindset doesn't help. Her choices matter: she can take a long walk, read something different to her usual genre, or let her mind wander without it feeling like failure.

The story

A quiet Tuesday

The idea comes on a quiet Tuesday. It arrives almost automatically, without any force. Her novel is about two characters who could not be more different. But, as they say, opposites attract. Their growth — painful, slow, real — is the story.

The story

The nightmare is over

When Maya finally reads the first chapter back, the feeling is overwhelming. It is good. It is really good. The nightmare is over.

Grammar Focus

Part 3 · three rules
Grammar

Every sentence needs a subject

Some languages drop the subject, because the verb ending shows who is acting. English cannot: the subject is ALWAYS there. Writes every morning is not a sentence. She writes every morning is.

With no real subject, English uses the dummy subject "it": It is true. It is exhausting to fight the block.

Grammar

Put the subject back

✓ SHE writes every morning.

A person does the action, so name the person.

✗ Writes every morning.

✓ IT is exhausting to fight the block.

The real subject, "to fight the block", moves to the end. "It" holds its place at the front.

✗ Is exhausting to fight the block.

✓ IT doesn't help to force ideas.

Nobody is doing this. "It" fills the empty subject position.

✗ Doesn't help to force ideas.

Grammar

Different from, to, than

All three exist, and they are not the same. DIFFERENT FROM is correct everywhere, so use it when in doubt.

Never "different of". It is a word-for-word translation, and it does not exist in English.

Grammar

Three forms, one safe choice

DIFFERENT FROM

British and American English. Always correct.

"Her style is different from mine."

DIFFERENT TO

British English only, and informal.

"This chapter is different to the last."

DIFFERENT THAN

American English, mostly before a clause.

"It's different than I expected."

Grammar

Present Simple negatives

SUBJECT + DON'T / DOESN'T + BASE VERB. DON'T with I, you, we, they. DOESN'T with he, she, it.

After DOESN'T the verb never takes -S: She doesn't write, not She doesn't writes. The -S is already in DOESN'T.

Grammar

DON'T or DOESN'T?

She DOESN'T write when she's overwhelmed.

"She" takes DOESN'T, and the verb stays "write".

Experts DON'T always agree.

"Experts" is plural, so DON'T.

The changes DON'T happen automatically.

A plural subject: DON'T + "happen", with no -S.

It DOESN'T help to force new ideas.

The dummy subject "it" takes DOESN'T, like "he" and "she".

Pronunciation

Part 4 · eleven words
Pronunciation

Silent letters, hidden sounds

nightmare · NIGHT-mare

Two syllables, stress on the first. The "gh" is silent: no /g/ and no /h/.

/ˈnaɪt.meə/

exhausting · ex-HAUST-ing

Three syllables, stress on the second. The "x" is /gz/ and the "h" is silent.

/ɪɡˈzɔː.stɪŋ/

experts · EX-perts

Stress on the first syllable, so the "x" is /ks/. When the stress falls just after "ex", as in "exhausting", it usually becomes /gz/.

/ˈek.spɜːts/

overwhelmed · o-ver-WHELMED

Stress on the last syllable. "wh" is just /w/, and -ED is only /d/: no extra syllable.

/ˌəʊ.vəˈwelmd/

Pronunciation

Where the stress falls

automatically · au-to-MAT-ic-ally

Five syllables, stress on the third. The first vowel is /ɔː/, as in "law", and "-ally" is said /li/.

/ˌɔː.təˈmæt.ɪ.kli/

consistent · con-SIS-tent

Stress on the middle syllable. The first and the last are weak: /kən/ and /tənt/.

/kənˈsɪs.tənt/

it is true · it-iz-TRUE

Linked in speech, as one word. The stress is on "true", and its vowel is long.

/ɪt ɪz truː/

changes · CHANGE-es

"ch" is /tʃ/, as in "church". -ES adds a syllable after the /dʒ/ sound.

/ˈtʃeɪn.dʒɪz/

Pronunciation

Endings

growth · GROWTH

One syllable, ending in /θ/, as in "think": the tongue tip between the teeth. Not /t/, /s/ or /f/.

/ɡrəʊθ/

fixed · FIXED

One syllable, ending /kst/. Do not add a vowel: never "fix-ed".

/fɪkst/

choices · CHOI-ces

"ch" is /tʃ/, the vowel is /ɔɪ/ as in "boy", and -ES adds a syllable.

/ˈtʃɔɪ.sɪz/

    🗣Speaking

    In pairs or small groups. Give examples, not one-word answers.

    • Tell your partner about a time you were blocked — in writing, music, work or study. What did it feel like, and what finally worked?
    • The story says that forcing ideas has the opposite effect. Argue for or against: can discipline produce creative work when inspiration is absent?
    • "Opposites attract" comes from magnets. Think of two more English expressions from science or nature, and use each in a sentence about creativity.
