The Writer's Nightmare:
Creativity, Block & the Blank Page

Read a short story about a novelist facing writer's block, master 10 key vocabulary items, study essential grammar rules for Spanish speakers, and sharpen your English pronunciation.

Part One
Read the Story

It is true — writer's block is a nightmare. Maya stares at the blank page every morning. She doesn't write a single word for days. The cursor blinks, consistent and merciless. It has the opposite effect: the more she tries to force ideas, the less creative she feels.

Her process is different from other writers she knows. Some authors say it is exhausting to fight the block. Experts say the changes in a writer's routine need to be gradual and intentional. A fixed mindset doesn't help. Choices matter — she can take a long walk, read something entirely different to her usual genre, or simply let her mind wander without it feeling like failure.

The idea comes on a quiet Tuesday. Almost automatically, without forcing anything. Her novel is about two characters who couldn't be more opposite in personality. But as they say: opposites attract. Their growth — painful, slow, real — is the story. And when Maya finally reads the first chapter back, it is overwhelmingly good. The nightmare is over.

Part Three
Grammar Focus

Three grammar rules that are especially important for Spanish speakers. Study each rule, then find examples in the reading passage above.

Rule A
Every English sentence needs a subject
In Spanish, the subject pronoun is optional because the verb ending tells you who is acting: "Escribe cada mañana" is perfectly correct. In English, the subject is always mandatory — the verb ending gives no information about the person.
✗  Writes every morning.  (missing subject)
✓  She writes every morning.
✗  Is exhausting to fight the block.
✓  It is exhausting to fight the block.
✗  Doesn't help to force ideas.
✓  It doesn't help to force ideas.
The dummy subject "it" — English uses it as a placeholder subject when there is no real person or thing. This is very common and almost invisible to English ears, but Spanish speakers often forget it entirely.
"It is true." / "It has the opposite effect." / "It is consistent with what experts say."
Rule B
Different from / to / than
All three forms exist, but their use depends on variety and context. The most important thing for Spanish speakers: never say "different of" — this is a direct mistranslation of diferente de.
FormVarietyExample
different from British & American — always correct "Her style is different from mine."
different to British English only "This chapter is different to the last."
different than American English — before a clause "It's different than I expected."
Common mistake: "Her writing is different of mine." — This does not exist in English. Use from when in doubt.
Rule C
Negative sentences in the Present Simple
Form: Subject + do not (don't) / does not (doesn't) + base verb
Use don't with I / you / we / they. Use doesn't with he / she / it.
Critical: after doesn't, the verb stays in its base form — never add -s.
✗  She doesn't writes when she's overwhelmed.
✓  She doesn't write when she's overwhelmed.
