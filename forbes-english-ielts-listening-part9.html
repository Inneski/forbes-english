How the Listening Test Works

Forty questions, heard once, no pausing. The rules, the question types, and the ways candidates lose marks they had already won.

C1 Advanced Listening · the rules 17 slides
The shape

Four sections, forty questions, one hearing

Four sections, ten questions each, one mark apiece. Sections 1 and 2 are everyday situations; 3 and 4 are study and lectures. The recording runs about thirty minutes.

You hear it once. You cannot pause it, rewind it, or ask for it again. And you will hear a range of accents, on purpose — coping with them is part of what is being tested.

One test for Academic and General Training, marked identically. Nothing on this page changes depending on which module you are sitting.

Paper or screen

One difference, and it is the clock

On paper. You write on the question paper as you listen, then get ten minutes at the end to copy your answers onto the answer sheet. Only the answer sheet is marked.

On screen. You type straight in, and you get two minutes at the end to check. Then the screen locks.

That is the only difference between the two. Same recording, same questions, same marking. If you are on paper, those ten minutes are not spare time — they are the transfer, and people run out of them.

What you will be asked

Five families of question

Completion. Sentence, summary, form, note, table, flow-chart: six forms of one task, a gap you fill.

Labelling. A diagram, or a map or plan. You match a name to a position.

Multiple choice. Sometimes one answer from three, sometimes two from five.

Matching and short answer. Options A–G matched to speakers or items — a Section 3 favourite — or a question answered in the words you heard.

The instructions change within a section. A word limit that applied to questions 1–5 may not apply to 6–10, and the limit is printed above each set, not once at the top.

Map and plan questions

The speaker walks; you follow

Six lettered points, and a voice walking past all of them. Orient yourself before the recording starts. Marks are lost here by losing your place, not by missing a word.

🗣Discussion · in pairs

Giving directions out loud is the fastest way to find out whether you can follow them.

  • Using the plan, describe a route past four of the six points. Your partner writes the letters in order, without seeing which you chose. Compare, then swap.
  • Do it again, but send your partner back to the junction halfway through. That reversal is the move that costs marks in the real test.
  • Describe your own street to your partner as if it were a plan. They sketch it. Then check the sketch against a map on your phone.
✍️Writing · 120–150 words

Write the directions a warden would give: from the car park, past four of the lettered points, ending at point F, at the far end of the east path. Then read it aloud and time it — around 150 words a minute is the pace of the real recording.
