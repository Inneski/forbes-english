Soundings of Frequency

Old sea charts mark the depth of the water in numbers called soundings. Here the depth is how often something happens: never on the dry shore, sometimes halfway out, always in the deepest water. The red buoy marks where the word drops anchor in a sentence. Read the depth, then drop the anchor.

Reading the depth

How often, and where it goes

An adverb of frequency answers How often? Choosing the word is half the job; the other half is the anchor, its place in the sentence.

Always and never are the ends

One is the deepest water, the other dry land. Everything else lies between.

The lighthouse is always on, but the ferry never runs at night.

Usually, often, sometimes

The middle depths: most of the time, a lot of the time, some of the time.

We usually sail on Saturdays, and sometimes on Sundays.

Rarely and hardly ever are already negative

They mean almost never, so they never take not: I hardly ever swim, not I don't hardly ever swim.

I hardly ever get seasick.

Before the main verb

With an ordinary verb, the adverb drops anchor just in front of it.

She often swims before breakfast.

After be

With am, is, are, was, were, it comes after the verb instead.

The sea is usually calm in July.

With two verbs: after the first

After have, can, will, must and the other helpers: I have never seen, you can always ask.

I have never seen a whale.

The eight soundings

The numbers are rough: often to one speaker is usually to another. What matters is the order.

The current

Front, middle and end

The middle of the sentence is always safe. A few adverbs can also ride the current to the front, for emphasis or contrast. Always and never stay anchored.

        The commonest mistakes with adverbs of frequency are about position, not meaning.

        Signal words

        Before the main verb

        • ordinary verbs: I often swim
        • he always waves
        • they never stop
        • in questions: Do you often swim?
        • in negatives: I don't often swim
        • with do / does / did doing the work

        After the first verb

        • be: she is often late
        • have: I have never seen
        • can: you can always ask
        • will: it will usually rain
        • must, should, could
        • the first verb carries the tense; the adverb follows it

        One test: find the verb that carries the tense. If it is be or a helper, the adverb comes after it; otherwise, before the main verb.
