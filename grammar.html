Grammar maps

Sailing the seas of grammar

Grammar drawn as a place: a coast for each pattern, a camp for each tense. Every chart lies rolled on the rack: point at one to unroll it (tap on a phone), then sail or climb to the practice.

  • Gerundia & Infinitivia · B1 · Free

    Gerunds or infinitives, coast by coast

    Every harbour on the west coast takes ‑ING, every harbour on the east takes TO + verb, and the island in the middle takes both, with a change of meaning. Learn the coastline and you know which form follows the verb.

    Set sail →

  • Regularia & Irregularia · A2–B1 · Free

    Regular and irregular verbs, island by island

    Almost every verb lives on one tidy continent and takes ‑ed, in one of three sounds. The rest live on islands, one family to an island, named after the sound they share: ring rang rung, keep kept, speak spoke spoken.

    Set sail →

  • Countania & Uncountania · A2–B1 · Free

    Countable and uncountable nouns, shore by shore

    A chair, two chairs, many chairs live on the west coast; furniture, much furniture, a piece of furniture live on the east. Below deck, the ship’s store cabin and cargo hold show the same cargo with two different words.

    Set sail →

  • The Adjective Canal · A2–B1 · Free

    Adjective order, lock by lock

    A noun travels with its adjectives like a barge down a canal: through eight locks, in one order, never backwards. Opinion first; purpose right next to the noun.

    Set sail →

  • The Prefix Archipelago · B1–B2 · Free

    Negative prefixes, island by island

    Most words sail to the mainland and take un-. The rest live on islands with their own rules: im- before p and m, il- before l, ir- before r, dis- for the opposite, mis- for wrongly.

    Set sail →

  • Soundings of Frequency · A2 · Free

    Adverbs of frequency, by the depth of the sea

    How often is how deep: never on the shore, always in the deepest water. The red buoy shows where the word drops anchor in a sentence.

    Set sail →

  • The Heights of Register · B2 · Free

    Formal and everyday words, by height

    Get on the shore, obtain near the summit: one meaning, two heights. The higher you climb, the more formal the writing, from chat and texts to reports and official letters.

    Set sail →

  • The Inversion Maelstrom · C1 · Free

    Negative inversion, rock by rock

    Sail past Never or Not only at the start of a sentence and the auxiliary spins in front of the subject: Never have I seen. No auxiliary? Do appears: Rarely do we sail at night.

    Set sail →

  • The Atlantic · B1–B2 · Free

    British and American English, shore to shore

    Elevator Harbour looks straight across at Lift Harbour. Eight word pairs, the grammar that differs, the words both shores share, and the ones that change meaning at sea.

  • The Return Voyage · B1 · Free

    Question tags, there and back

  • The Grammar Forecast · B1–B2 · Free

    Probability, by the weather
