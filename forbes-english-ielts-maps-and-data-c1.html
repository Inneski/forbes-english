Describing Maps: Location & Change Vocabulary

Maps appear less often than charts in Task 1, but examiners expect the same precision: compass points, the right verb for position, and paraphrases that don't cross a line the language won't allow.

Reading the map before you write

Four rules from the lesson that examiners check closely.

Position

"is located" is not past tense

It's passive voice, present tense. Vary it with "is situated" or "lies" — never change it to "locates".

Paraphrase limits

A town is never a city

"Town centre" can only become "the centre of the town" — "city centre" is factually wrong if the map says town.

Paraphrase limits

Roads stay roads

If the key says "road", write "road" — not "street" or "lane". Each names a different kind of route, and the examiner checks your words against the map.

The key

Capitals in the key, lower case in your writing

Keys often label features in capitals. Write them as ordinary prose: HOUSING AREA becomes housing area — but DARTFORD becomes Dartford, because a name keeps its capital.

Accurate Data Language

When a chart doesn't give you a clean number, an examiner still expects precision — not a made-up figure. This module drills the five-band vocabulary of approximation.

Five bands of precision

Anchor every expression to how far the value sits from the reference number.

Well under

well under / considerably less than

A value a long way below the marker.

Just under

just under / slightly under / almost / nearly

A value only a little below the marker.

Unclear direction

about / approximately / around / more or less / close to

Use this band only when you genuinely can't tell if it's above or below.

Just over & well over

just over / slightly over / marginally above — well over / considerably more than

Mirror the "under" bands on the other side of the marker.
