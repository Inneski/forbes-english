The moon · the causative

Somebody else did it, and you arranged it

Not a tense but a shape, and one English uses constantly. You stay the subject — it was your decision and your money — while the hands belong to a hairdresser, a garage or a roofer who never makes it into the sentence at all. The whole trick is where you put the thing.

Examples in
Moon briefing

When to use it

The causative is the sentence for work you arrange rather than do. You are still the subject — it is your money and your decision — but the hands belong to somebody you usually do not bother to name.

A service you pay for

The classic use, and the reason it exists.

"I had my hair cut on Friday."

Work on a house, a car, a tooth

Anywhere a professional does the thing to your thing.

"We had the kitchen rewired."

Get instead of have

More informal, and slightly more effort on your part.

"I must get this jacket cleaned."

Something bad that happened to you

Here you arranged nothing. This is the other causative.

"They had their car stolen."

Making somebody do it

With a person and no to: this one is about authority.

"She had the porter carry the bags."

Persuading somebody to do it

Get + person takes to. Have never does.

"I got my brother to help."
Building it

How it's built

The everyday one

subject + have + thing + past participle

"I had the car serviced."

The informal one

subject + get + thing + past participle

"I got the car serviced."

With a person, using have

subject + have + person + infinitive

"He had the plumber look at it."

With a person, using get

subject + get + person + to + infinitive

"He got the plumber to look at it."

So is this the passive?

Half of it is, which is why the causative sits on the descent side of the mountain rather than up with the tenses. The main clause is active — you are the subject and you did the arranging. What follows have is a passive in miniature: my hair cut is my hair [was] cut with the auxiliary squeezed out. Many coursebooks call it the causative passive for exactly that reason.

Part of the sentenceVoiceWhy
I had …ActiveYou are the subject and the arranger.
… my hair cutPassiveThe hair is having it done to it, by somebody unnamed.
My hair was cut.PassiveThe full passive — and it loses you entirely.
I cut my hair.ActiveThe plain active — and now you held the scissors.

The causative is the sentence English uses when neither plain voice will do: the passive loses you, the active makes you the barber. This one keeps you responsible and still hands the work to somebody else — which is exactly what happened.

The word order is the whole trick

The thing comes before the participle, never after it. Put the participle next to have and you have written the present perfect instead, which says something completely different about who did the work.

SentenceMeansWho did it
I had cut my hair.Present perfect: I did it myself, earlier.You, with scissors.
I had my hair cut.Causative: I arranged it.A hairdresser.
I have repaired the roof.I climbed up and repaired it.You.
I have had the roof repaired.I paid somebody to.A roofer.

It moves through every tense

Only have changes. The thing and the participle behind it never move, so once you can hear the order you can build it in any tense on the mountain.

TenseExampleCamp
Present simpleI have my hair cut every month.Camp two
Past simpleI had my hair cut on Friday.Camp three
Present perfectI have had my hair cut.Camp four
FutureI'll have my hair cut before the wedding.The moon
ContinuousI'm having my hair cut at three.Camp one

Three mistakes worth naming

Almost every error with the causative is one of these three.

Not thisThisWhy
I had cut my hair at the salon.I had my hair cut at the salon.The thing goes between had and the participle. Otherwise you cut it yourself.
I had the plumber to look at it.I had the plumber look at it.Have + person takes the bare infinitive. Only get takes to.
I had my hair cutted.I had my hair cut.It is the third form, and for cut that is cut.
The fork in the path

Have or get?

They are close enough that most of the time either works. Where they differ is in how much effort the sentence implies on your part, and what follows when a person is involved.

Questionhaveget
RegisterNeutral, and slightly more formal.Informal, and more common in speech.
With a person?Bare infinitive: "have him look".Takes to: "get him to look".
Any effort implied?None. You simply arranged it.Some. You had to persuade or chase.
Something bad?Yes: "they had their car stolen".Yes: "he got his phone taken".

The pair that shows the order: "I had cut my hair" — I stood in front of a mirror with scissors. "I had my hair cut" — I sat in a chair and paid. Three words, same words, and the only difference is where my hair sits.

Where you will meet it

  • at the hairdresser's
  • at the garage
  • at the dentist's
  • building work · repairs
  • printing · framing · dry cleaning
  • anything you pay somebody to do

The other causative — nothing arranged

  • have your car stolen
  • have your phone taken
  • have your flight cancelled
  • get your fingers burnt
  • have your wallet lifted
  • have your hours cut

A useful habit: if you can put somebody else did it, and I organised it after the sentence and it still makes sense, the causative is the form you want.
