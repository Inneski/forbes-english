Active & Passive

Who did it, who it was done to, and when English decides not to say

B1 · Intermediate The passive voice 19 slides
Before the questions

Two voices, one event

Active: the subject acts

Subject, verb, object. Lego releases new sets every year. The subject does the releasing, and this is the ordinary way English puts a sentence together.

Direct, short, and the right default. The passive is the marked choice.

Passive: the subject receives

Be + past participle. New sets are released every year. The subject is on the receiving end, and the doer can be named with by or left out entirely.

Same event, different thing in the spotlight.

Three steps to turn one into the other

The object becomes the subject; the verb becomes be + past participle in the original tense; the old subject becomes by + agent, and that part is optional.

Ole Kirk Christiansen founded Lego → Lego was founded by Ole Kirk Christiansen.

The mechanism

Be carries the tense, always

The simple tenses

Present: Lego makes bricks → bricks are made. Past: they built a castle → a castle was built. Only be moves; the participle never changes.

Is / are for the present, was / were for the past.

The perfect tenses

She has tested the set → the set has been tested. He had launched it → it had been launched. The perfect keeps its auxiliary and adds been.

Has been, had been — two words, and both are needed.

Modals and the infinitive

After a modal, be stays bare: you can assemble it → it can be assembled. The passive infinitive is to be + past participle: designs that might be turned into sets.

A modal never takes been. Can been assembled is not English.

Why bother

When the passive is the better sentence

When nobody knows who

The brick was dropped. If the doer is unknown, or obvious, or beside the point, the active forces you to invent a subject. The passive lets you leave it out.

It is not evasion. Often it is simply the only honest sentence available.

When the result is the news

Reports, labels, instructions and science put the thing first: the design was patented in 1958. The patent office is not what the sentence is about.

This is why formal writing uses it so much — and why it can sound cold.

The three mistakes

Never was been — it is either was or has been. Agree with the new subject: the bricks were made. And use the participle, not the past: was written, not was wrote.

Every one of these comes from forgetting that be is the verb doing the work.

Activation

Write the museum label

Use at least four: was made is kept has been used can be seen by the designer it was decided are believed to was given to me
🗣Roleplay · in pairs

One of you is a museum guide, the other a visitor who keeps asking “but who actually did it?”. Three minutes each, then swap.

  • Describe how something in the room was made, without saying who made it.
  • Your partner asks who is responsible. Answer twice: once naming the agent, once leaving it out.
  • Explain a rule at your school or workplace using the passive three times.
  • Tell the story of an object you own — where it was made, when it was given to you, what has been done to it since.
✍️Writing · 120–150 words

Write the label a museum would put beside a famous object. Say when it was made, what it was made from, how it has been used and where it is kept now. Name an agent only where the reader would actually want to know.
