Work, Automation and Cities

Three topics that come up again and again in Part 3 and Task 2, and the pairings that make an answer on any of them sound thought through

C1 · Advanced Speaking & Writing Task 2 18 points
Idea one

Work: flexibility against security

The pairings

Job security, career prospects, a work–life balance: nouns that arrive with their partner fixed. You work remotely on a flexible schedule, you suffer burnout, a firm takes on staff. None of the words is rare. The pairings are what English says.

Learn suffer burnout. Not burnout.

Flexibility against isolation

The Part 3 question is nearly always a form of is working from home good for people? The answer that scores holds two pairings against each other: a flexible schedule on one side, the colleagues you no longer see on the other.

One idea, two phrases, and but between them.

Security against opportunity

The gig economy is behind many of the Task 2 questions on work. Argue it as a trade: it offers a flexible schedule and takes away job security; it widens career prospects for some and narrows them for others.

Say what it gives and what it takes. That is the paragraph.

Idea two

Automation: jobs lost against jobs created

The pairings

Automate routine tasks, replace workers, retrain the workforce; productivity gains, low-skilled jobs, artificial intelligence. The noun brings its verb: tasks are automated, workers are displaced, a workforce is retrained.

The verb is automate, not automatise.

Lost against created

Every automation prompt is the same trade: machines displace workers in low-skilled jobs, and the productivity gains pay for jobs that did not exist before. An answer with only the first half is a band 6. The one that weighs both is what Part 3 is asking for.

Name who loses, name who gains, and say which matters more.

Who retrains whom

The second argument is about responsibility. Should the state retrain the workforce, or the employer that replaced it? A universal basic income is the phrase for the answer that says neither.

One pairing per position, and three positions to choose from.

Idea three

Cities: density against space, the car against the bus

The pairings

Urban sprawl, affordable housing, public transport, green spaces, the cost of living. A council invests in infrastructure and tries to ease congestion; a resident has a long commute and puts up with overcrowding.

Congestion is eased. Traffic is heavy, never strong.

Density against space

The Task 2 prompt is usually a version of should cities build up or out? Up means affordable housing near the centre and less urban sprawl, at the price of overcrowding. Out means green spaces and a long commute.

Both answers cost something. Say what.

The car against the bus

Part 3 asks how to ease congestion. The answer that has thought about it before says a city cannot build its way out, because every new road fills; so the money goes to public transport instead. Invest in infrastructure is the pairing that carries the point.

The cost of living closes either answer: whoever pays, it is the resident.

    Answer from the bank

    Use at least three: job security take on staff retrain the workforce productivity gains affordable housing ease congestion
    🗣Discussion · in pairs

    In pairs. One of you is the examiner and asks Part 3 questions on work and cities — is working from home good for a company? Should cities build up or out? Who should pay to retrain people? The other answers each one for a minute using only the bank on this deck. Then swap.

    • Every answer holds two pairings against each other — one for each side, with but or whereas between them.
    • The examiner stops you the moment you use a bare word where the bank has a pairing, and you say it again with the phrase.
    • Every third question is a follow-up — why? or can you give an example? — and the answer has to add a pairing you have not used yet.
    ✍️Writing · 150–200 words

    Choose one of the three ideas and write a Task 2 body paragraph on it: the claim, the argument, an example, the concession. Use at least five pairings from the bank and underline each one. Then read it back and count: a sentence with no pairing in it is doing nothing for your Lexical Resource.
