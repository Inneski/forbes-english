Before you start

Talking about figures: three words, three jobs

In, of, by

A rise in demand names what is growing. An increase of twelve percent gives the size. Costs dropping by a fifth gives the size of the change.

All three can appear in one sentence, each doing a different job.

From here to there

From X to Y marks the two ends of a change — the fleet grew from nine vans to forty. The starting point comes first.

The size of a change takes by after a verb (rose by 500) and of after a noun (an increase of 500). From…to names the two ends instead.

Comparing and qualifying

Compared to sets two things side by side. A shortage of names what is missing. With regard to narrows to one aspect.

With regard to is formal and singular — never with regards to, which is a sign-off.

Before you start

More fixed phrases, and the trap inside them

The in family

In charge of, in favour of, in return for, in addition to. Four phrases that all open with in — which is why the first half is rarely the difficult part.

If the phrase describes a role or a position, the odds favour in.

States, not places

Out of the question, under control, beyond doubt. Each describes a condition something is in, and none of them is literal.

Beyond means past the point where it could be argued — the same beyond as in beyond repair.

The tail is not always of

In charge of, in favour of, but in return for and in addition to. Learning the opening preposition is only half the phrase.

This is why they are memorised whole rather than by rule.

Before you start

More dependent pairs, including one real trap

When to is not an infinitive

You object to something, and that to is a preposition — so a verb after it takes -ing. The neighbours objected to the trees being cut down, never to be cut down.

The same trap sits inside look forward to and be used to.

Trusting and mentioning

Rely on someone to do something. Refer to something in a speech or a text. Both fixed, neither negotiable.

Refer to also means "look something up", with the same preposition.

Leaving, gaining, boasting

You resign from a post and benefit from a scheme — both take from. You boast about something, and excuse someone for it.

Two verbs sharing a preposition is the exception here, not the rule.

Before you start

Handling, recovering, delaying, bumping into

Both take with

You deal with a problem and you cope with pressure. Deal with is about solving it; cope with is about surviving it.

A manager deals with a conflict. The team copes with the workload.

Getting past it, or putting it off

Get over something is to recover from it emotionally. Put off something is to postpone it — and it is the decision, not the person, that gets put off.

Put someone off exists too, and means to discourage them. Different verb.

By chance, or behind

Come across a thing and run into a person — both mean by chance, but one is for objects and one is for people. Fall behind is to slip back against a plan.

You come across a photo; you run into an old teacher.

Present the figures

🗣Roleplay · in pairs

One of you presents this quarter’s results to the board; the other is a board member who does not accept them. Four minutes each, then swap.

  • Describe one number that went up and one that went down, using a rise in, an increase of and fall by correctly.
  • Say how this quarter looks compared to the last, and what you are still in charge of fixing.
  • Push back: ask what is being done about the one figure that is not yet under control.
