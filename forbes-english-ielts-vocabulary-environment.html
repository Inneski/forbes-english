Idea 1 of 3

Emissions: four pairings and two arguments

Cut them — the noun picks the verb

Emissions are cut, reduced or curbed; a country burns fossil fuels and releases greenhouse gases; a city measures its air quality. The verb is chosen by the noun, and none of these is rare.

Four pairings, learnt whole. A bare emissions in the notebook is worth none of them.

The footprint and the tax

A person or a firm has a carbon footprint, and shrinking it is what a carbon tax is for. Both are fixed: the mark you leave is a footprint, not a trace, and the charge is a tax, not a fine.

File them together. The tax exists because of the footprint.

The argument it arrives with

Two arguments come up again and again here. Who pays: dirty air lands on people who did not produce it. Targets against enforcement: a country can promise to halve its emissions and never fine a single factory.

File the idea, and the paragraph is half-written before the question is read.

Idea 2 of 3

Renewables: generate, subsidise, phase out

Generate: the verb to reach for

A wind farm or a roof of solar panels generates electricity, and what it generates feeds the national grid. Renewable energy is the umbrella term; renewables on its own is the noun a Part 3 answer uses.

Four pairings and one umbrella noun. All plain, none of them rare.

The precise word for the weakness

The sun sets and the wind drops, so the supply is intermittent: it comes and goes on its own schedule. Several words sound close and mean something else, and the examiner counts the one that means what you meant.

A near-miss that sounds advanced is still a miss; the plain word that lands is not.

The argument it arrives with

Governments subsidise renewables and phase out coal, and most essays on the topic weigh the same two tensions. Reliability against cost: cheap to generate, dear to store. Subsidy against the market: the panels exist because someone paid, and may need someone to keep paying.

Energy security is the third term: a country that generates its own power cannot be cut off.

Idea 3 of 3

Consumption and waste: the pairing includes the preposition

Sent to landfill, cut down on

Rubbish is sent to landfill; a council reports its recycling rates; a shopper cuts down on excess packaging. The preposition belongs to the pairing: in British English landfill takes no article (American English says a landfill), and the phrasal verb is two particles, not one.

Write the whole thing. Half a phrasal verb is a miss.

Single-use, throwaway, deposit

A bottle used once is single-use plastic; a society that expects to bin things has a throwaway culture; a scheme that pays you to bring the bottle back is a deposit scheme. Two adjective-noun pairings and a compound noun, none of them rare.

A throwaway culture says more than consumerism: it names the habit, not just the system.

The argument it arrives with

Ask who should change and the answer is written. Individual against producer responsibility: a shopper can change consumer habits, but the packaging was decided before the shopper arrived. Convenience against cost: single-use is cheap now and paid for later.

Both arguments run either way round, which is what makes them worth filing.

    In pairs. One of you is the examiner and asks three Part 3 questions on the environment — whether governments or individuals should act on emissions, whether renewables can replace coal, whether recycling is worth the effort. The other answers using pairings from the bank. Swap after three.

    • Every answer carries at least one pairing from the bank, said whole — the verb with its noun, or the adjective with its noun.
    • The examiner asks why after every answer. The follow-up has to use one of the arguments the idea arrived with: who pays, reliability against cost, individual against producer.
    • If the word is not in the bank, describe the thing in plain English rather than reaching for a rare word.
