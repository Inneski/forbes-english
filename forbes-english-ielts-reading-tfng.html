True, False, Not Given

The question type candidates find hardest to call — and one where technique wins marks back

C1 · Advanced Reading · both modules 18 points
Before you start

Sixty minutes, three sections, no time at the end

The shape of the paper

Forty questions in sixty minutes, in three sections of about twenty minutes each. The third is usually the hardest — so a section that runs long is borrowing from the one that needs it most.

Academic sets one long passage per section; General Training starts with shorter texts. The technique is the same, so everything here applies to both.

No transfer time. None.

On paper, Listening gives you ten minutes at the end to copy your answers across. Reading does not. Write on the answer sheet as you go, because the invigilator stops you on the hour with whatever is on it.

Answers left on the question paper score nothing. It is the single cheapest way to lose marks on this paper.

The questions follow the text

Most question types run in passage order, this one included. Answer four and you know roughly where the fifth is — you never have to search the whole passage twice.

Matching Information and Matching Features jump around, and in Matching Headings the list of headings has no order.

Before you start

False and Not Given are not the same answer

FALSE: the passage says otherwise

There is a sentence in the text that contradicts the statement. You can put your finger on it. If someone asked you to prove the statement wrong, you would point at that line.

A number that disagrees, a date that does not match, an "only" against a "several" — all contradictions.

NOT GIVEN: the passage is silent

The text neither says it nor denies it. The statement may well be true out in the world; the passage simply does not go there.

Not Given is not a punishment for missing something. It is a real answer, and you should expect several on a paper.

One question decides it

Can I point at the sentence? If yes, the answer is True or False depending on what it says. If you are filling a gap the passage leaves — “well, it must be” — the answer is Not Given.

Filling the gap feels like understanding, which is exactly why it costs so many marks here.

Before you start

Answer from the passage, never from what you know

Your knowledge is the trap

A statement can be perfectly true in the world and still Not Given in the text. The examiner is not asking whether it is true. They are asking what this passage says.

This is why a familiar topic can cost you marks here.

TRUE means the text says it

Said in different words, almost always. The passage will not repeat the statement; it will paraphrase it. Matching meaning is the skill — matching words is a habit to break.

Attendance fell sharply after 1990 and far fewer people came in the years that followed are the same claim with no word in common.

Word-matching fails both ways

The same words can sit in a sentence that says the opposite, and a sentence with no shared words at all can state the claim exactly. Shared vocabulary tells you where to look, never what to answer.

Use the repeated word to find the line. Then read the line.

Before you start

One word can decide it

Absolutes

All, every, never, only. A passage that says most makes a statement saying all FALSE: most tells you not all, because a writer who meant all would say so.

Most islanders against every islander is a False, not a Not Given — unless the passage says most, if not all.

Hedges

May, might, is thought to, suggests. A hedged passage does not deny a confident statement — it just never makes it. That shape is usually Not Given.

The difference from an absolute: most tells you not all, but may tells you nothing either way.

Comparisons need both sides

Wetter than, the largest, more common than. Check that the passage actually compares the things the statement compares — a text about one of them cannot support a claim about how they rank.

A figure for only one of the two, with nothing to compare it with, cannot support a ranking.

    🗣Discussion · in pairs

    In pairs, with any passage you have to hand — a news article will do. One of you writes four statements about it: one true, one false, two not given. Swap and answer. Defend each TRUE or FALSE by reading out the line it rests on; for a NOT GIVEN, show that there is none.

    • Whoever answers must say which sentence decided it, out loud, before the verdict is accepted.
    • For every NOT GIVEN, say what the passage would have had to contain for the answer to be FALSE instead.
    • Find one statement in your partner’s set that you could argue either way, and rewrite it so that it cannot be.
    ✍️Writing · 150–200 words

    Take one passage and write three statements about it — one TRUE, one FALSE, one NOT GIVEN — then write the answer key, naming for each one the exact sentence that decides it, or saying plainly that no sentence does.
