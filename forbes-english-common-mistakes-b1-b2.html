Grammar Notebook

Past Simple – negative
SUBJECT + DIDN'T + BASE VERB
I didn't know about that when I had my son.
I wasn't know about this.
"Didn't" already carries the past tense, so the verb after it stays in its base form: know, go, do, see.
"In sooth I know not why I am so sad. It wearies me, you say it wearies you."
Shakespeare, The Merchant of Venice — "I know not" is an old-fashioned form of "I don't know." We don't use it in modern speech, but it shows the same idea: the negative word does the work, the verb stays simple.
Do vs. Make
to DO sports / to DO homework / to MAKE a decision / to MAKE a mistake
I do sports every weekend.
I make sports every weekend.
"Make" is for creating or producing something. "Do" is for activities and tasks — including sport.
Questions with a negative
WHAT + DIDN'T + SUBJECT + BASE VERB?
What didn't I do?
What didn't I not do?
English only needs one negative. Adding a second "not" cancels the meaning instead of strengthening it.
Talking about temperature
IT + WAS/IS + [number] DEGREES
It was 38 degrees yesterday.
We had 38 degrees yesterday.
Weather uses "it": it was hot, it was raining, it was 38 degrees. English doesn't "have" a temperature.
Relative pronouns: that vs. what
EVERYTHING + THAT + SUBJECT + VERB
Release everything that you knew before.
Release everything what you knew before.
"What" already means "the thing that," so it can't follow a noun like "everything." Use "that" (or "which") after a noun.
Uncountable nouns
SOME / MUCH / A LOT OF + INFORMATION (no plural)
I need some information.
I need some informations.
"Information" has no plural form in English, just like "advice," "furniture," and "news."
Asking someone to do something for you
WILL + YOU + BASE VERB...?
Will you send it to me?
Do you send it to me?
"Do you send" asks about a habit. To make a request about one specific future action, use "will."
Similar to (not "with")
SIMILAR + TO

Second Conditional
IF + SUBJECT + PAST SIMPLE, WOULD + INFINITIVE
If I bought shoes for Mila, it would make her very happy.
If I would buy shoes, I would make her a pleasure.
Use the second conditional for an imagined or unlikely present/future situation. "Would" only ever appears in the result clause, never after "if." Note the vocabulary: to make someone happy, to be pleased, and to be a pleasure (e.g. "It was a pleasure meeting you").
