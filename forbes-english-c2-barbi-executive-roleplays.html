The COO Briefings

Six situations pulled straight from your world — escalations, intakes, partnerships, coaching, bad news, and the boardroom. Each dossier arms you with three advanced C2 phrases. Your mission: land them naturally, under pressure.

How to run a dossier

  1. Read the setting and your brief. Tap each target phrase to study it before you begin.
  2. Your teacher plays the person across the table — and they will not make it easy.
  3. Improvise the conversation. Work all three phrases in without them sounding forced.
  4. Feeling confident? Press Raise the stakes mid-scene for a complication.
  5. Debrief, then stamp the dossier Played.

RP-01 · Client Operations Played

The Friday Escalation Call

Setting A 30-minute emergency video call, late on a Friday. The VP of Engineering at a US healthtech scale-up is furious: your engineering pod missed a critical release, and he is openly questioning whether the partnership is worth it.
Across the table (your teacher plays) The VP — frustrated, interrupting, and looking for someone to blame.
Your brief
  • Let him vent, then take ownership without throwing your engineers under the bus.
  • Present a concrete recovery plan with dates and named owners.
  • Close the issue firmly so it does not poison the next quarter.
Target phrases — tap to study
to smooth ruffled feathers
To calm someone who has been annoyed or offended, and repair the relationship.
"Before we talk process, let me spend a minute smoothing ruffled feathers — your team had every right to be frustrated on Tuesday."
to fall on your sword
To take full personal responsibility for a failure, especially to protect your team.
"I am happy to fall on my sword here — the staffing decision on that sprint was mine, not the engineers'."
to draw a line under something
To declare a difficult matter finished so that everyone can move forward.
"Once the recovery plan ships next Friday, I would like us to draw a line under this and refocus on the roadmap."
Complication Mid-call, the VP mentions — almost casually — that he has already requested quotes from a rival LATAM staffing firm. React without panicking, and without matching a price you have not seen.
RP-02 · Recruiting Played

The Impossible Intake

Setting An intake meeting with the CTO of a fintech startup, a brand-new client. She wants a full-stack engineer who is also an ML specialist, a DevOps expert and a team lead — at mid-level rates, hired within two weeks.
Across the table (your teacher plays) The CTO — brilliant, impatient, and convinced this person exists.
Your brief
  • Take her requirements seriously — then reshape them into a search your team can actually win.
  • Persuade her to split the role or adjust the compensation band.
  • Leave the meeting with her trust intact and a realistic timeline agreed.
Target phrases — tap to study
to square the circle
To attempt something that seems impossible because it involves two contradictory demands.
"Senior ML expertise at mid-level rates in two weeks — honestly, that is asking us to square the circle. Let me show you two ways to make this solvable."
to rein in
To bring something ambitious or excessive under control; to limit it.
"If we rein in the must-have list to four core skills, the candidate pool goes from three people in all of LATAM to about two hundred."
to bring someone round
To gradually persuade someone to accept your point of view.
"Her first reaction was a hard no, but by the end of the call I had brought her round to a two-hire structure."
Complication She tells you a competitor has promised her a shortlist in five days. Do you match the promise, challenge it, or reframe what a good shortlist looks like?
RP-03 · Partnerships Played

The Partnership Dinner

Setting Dinner at a conference with the founder of a fast-growing payroll and EOR platform for Latin America. He is proposing a referral partnership: his clients need engineers, your clients need payroll. On paper, a perfect fit.
Across the table (your teacher plays) The founder — charming, generous with the wine, and moving faster than you would like.
Your brief
  • State clearly what South Geeks brings and what you expect in return.
  • Explore the opportunity warmly — but commit to nothing tonight.
  • Protect your margins and your existing partner relationships.
Target phrases — tap to study
to set out your stall
To present your position, strengths and intentions clearly and confidently.
"Let me set out our stall: we place elite engineering talent, we do it fast, and we only partner where the incentives point the same way."
to overplay your hand
To push an advantage too far and end up weakening your own position.
"I love the ambition, but if we demand exclusivity from day one we risk overplaying our hand with partners we both need."
to steal a march on
To gain an advantage over rivals by acting before they do.
"Done well, this alliance lets both of us steal a march on the bigger players before they even notice the gap."
Complication Over dessert, he asks for exclusivity — tonight, with a handshake. Decline gracefully without cooling the relationship.
RP-04 · People Ops Played

The Coaching Conversation

Setting A one-to-one with a recruiting manager who has been with the company for years. She is openly resisting the new AI sourcing tools, her team is following her lead, and their pipeline metrics are slipping.
Across the table (your teacher plays) The manager — proud of her track record, defensive, and quietly worried.
Your brief
  • Name the problem plainly — no hiding behind small talk.
  • Honour her experience genuinely, not with hollow compliments.
  • Get her to champion the tools with her team, and agree concrete next steps.
Target phrases — tap to study
to grasp the nettle
To tackle an unpleasant problem directly and bravely instead of avoiding it.
"I would rather grasp the nettle now than watch your team's numbers slide for another quarter — so let's talk about what is really going on."
to dig your heels in
To refuse stubbornly to change your position or behaviour.
"From where I sit, it looks like the team has dug its heels in on the new tools — help me understand what is driving that."
to win hearts and minds
To persuade people to support something willingly, emotionally as well as rationally.
"I do not need compliance from your team — I need you to win hearts and minds, because you are the one they trust."
Complication Her voice cracks: "Be honest with me, Barbi — these tools are here to replace people like me, aren't they?" Answer truthfully, warmly, and without making promises you cannot keep.
RP-05 · Delivery Played

Losing the Finalist

Setting A call you have been dreading. After a six-week interview process, the client's favourite candidate has just accepted a faster offer elsewhere. You have to break the news — and the slow process was the client's own doing.
Across the table (your teacher plays) The hiring manager — disappointed, and looking for somewhere to put the blame.
Your brief
  • Deliver the news cleanly — no burying it in pleasantries.
  • Soften the blow without disguising the cause.
  • Push firmly for a two-week process commitment on the next search.
Target phrases — tap to study
to take the sting out of
To make something painful or disappointing easier to accept.
"Nothing takes the sting out of losing him entirely, but I have two strong candidates who can be in final rounds by Thursday."
to be on the back foot
To be in a defensive or disadvantaged position.
"With a six-week process in this market, we were on the back foot from the second interview onwards."
to hold someone's feet to the fire
To put pressure on someone to honour a commitment or take responsibility.
"On the next search I will hold your team's feet to the fire on the timeline — and you should hold mine on candidate quality."
Complication The hiring manager snaps back: "Your team should have kept him warm. This is on South Geeks." Push back with facts, not apology — and keep the relationship.
RP-06 · Leadership Played

The Quarterly Review

Setting The quarterly leadership meeting. You are presenting operations results — including one weak number — and proposing a significant investment in AI tooling. The mood in the room is cost-cutting.
Across the table (your teacher plays) The founders and a sceptical head of finance who would rather delay everything.
Your brief
  • Present the weak number honestly — cosmetic spin will cost you credibility.
  • Commit publicly and personally to the AI investment.
  • Refuse to let the decision be postponed into vagueness.
Target phrases — tap to study
to nail your colours to the mast
To declare your position openly and commit to it, whatever the consequences.
"I will nail my colours to the mast: I believe this tooling pays for itself within three quarters, and I am willing to be measured on it."
to kick something into the long grass
To postpone a decision indefinitely in the hope it will be forgotten.
"If we kick this into the long grass for two more quarters, our competitors will make the decision for us."
to paper over the cracks
To hide problems superficially instead of fixing them.
"I could paper over the cracks in the delivery numbers, but you hired me to tell you the truth — so here it is."
Complication The head of finance proposes freezing all new spend for two quarters — "including this AI experiment." The room goes quiet and everyone looks at you.

Debrief questions for after each scene

  • Which phrase felt natural — and which one did you have to force?
  • What would you say differently if the conversation were in front of your own team?
  • Where did you soften your message more than you needed to?
  • Replay the hardest 30 seconds of the scene — but this time, say less.
