Before you listen

One voice, and almost nothing said twice

Nobody asks a question

In Section 1 the other speaker keeps checking — sorry, forty-two? — and every check is a free repeat. In a monologue nobody does that, so a detail you miss is usually gone.

That is why the preparation time matters so much here.

The map task is about orientation as much as English

Labelling a plan tests whether you can hold an orientation while somebody walks you through a space in words. The vocabulary is small; the difficulty is that everything is relative to something else.

In the real paper you get a drawn plan with letters on it. Here the places and positions are matched instead — the same work, one step before the letters.

Fix your starting point first

On your left as you come through the gate means nothing until you know where the gate is and which way you are facing. Find the entrance on the plan before the recording starts, and mark which way is "ahead".

Many map-task marks are lost by candidates who never worked out where they were standing.

After the recording

How a map task hides its answers

The shared landmark

Two things are put beside the same feature — the play area and the bicycle racks are both next to the car park. Naming the landmark does not identify either of them, so the sentence has to be held whole.

Two things or in fact can signal that a second item is about to share a position.

The thing that moved

Something is described in its old place and then corrected to its new one. The plant stall "used to stand by the lake". Anyone answering from the first mention puts it in the wrong place.

The guide repeats the new position deliberately. In a monologue, a repeat is usually a signal.

The direction that depends on you

On your left, directly ahead, behind the lake, on the far side from where we are standing. Every one of these is relative to the speaker’s position, not to the page.

Behind and beyond are the two that catch people: behind the lake is on its far side, beyond the greenhouse is past it.

    Activation

    Give the tour

    Use at least three: fix the entrance first on your left behind = far side beyond = past it two things, one landmark a repeat is a flag
    🗣Discussion · in pairs

    In pairs, with a simple plan each — draw the room you are in, or a building you both know. One of you describes where five things are, without pointing and without naming a compass direction. The other marks them on their own copy and then compares.

    • Describer: fix the starting point out loud first. "You come in through the door by the lifts." Then everything is relative to that.
    • Put two of your five things beside the same landmark, and see whether your partner separates them.
    • Move one thing halfway through: "the printer used to be by the window — it is now…" Say the new place twice, as the guide in the recording does.
    ✍️Writing · 100–150 words

    Write the opening ninety seconds of a tour of somewhere you know well, describing where five things are. Use no compass directions at all — only positions relative to the entrance and to each other — and put two of them beside the same landmark.
