Escape from Alcatraz

Five locked rooms, five pieces of English. Find the numbers, open the last gate.

A2 · Elementary Escape room 50–60 minutes 43 slides
Before you start

Welcome to The Rock

Alcatraz was a prison on a small island in San Francisco Bay. It opened in 1934 and closed in 1963.

The island is about 1.25 miles (2 km) from the city. People called it The Rock.

In twenty-nine years there were fourteen escape attempts. No prisoner is officially recorded as escaping successfully.

The water is cold — about 10–13°C — and the currents are strong.

One man, John Paul Scott, did reach land by swimming in December 1962. He was found on the shore, cold and exhausted, and taken back.

Before you start

Tonight you are going to leave

You are a prisoner in B Block. Tonight you have one night and five rooms: your cell, the corridor, the barber shop, the workshop and the water.

In every room you find one number. The five numbers open the last gate.

The rail at the bottom of the screen remembers the numbers for you, so you can think about the English instead.

Some rooms have a clock. When you see the clock, look fast.

Everything in this lesson happened on a real island. The prisoner is invented; the escape in 1962 was not.

Room 1 · Your cell

Where is it? in, on, under, behind

Use these words to say where something is: in, on, under, behind, next to, between, above.

The spoon is under the bed. The vent is behind the sink. The photo is on the wall.

After the preposition comes the and the thing. Never add to.

✓ The light is above the door.
✗ The light is above to the door.

Next to is two words. Between needs two things and the word and.

Room 2 · The corridor

There is one. There are two.

One thing: There is. More than one: There are.

There is a ladder. There are three pipes.

Questions and negatives keep the same pattern.

Is there a guard? — No, there isn’t. Are there any lights? — No, there aren’t.

Water, air, noise and light have no plural form: say there is some water, never there are some waters.

Room 3 · The barber shop

What they did: the past simple

For finished actions in the past, use the past simple. Many common verbs are irregular, so you have to learn them.

make → made · take → took · get → got · go → went · put → put · cut → cut

In negatives and questions use did, and then the base form of the verb.

They didn’t take the boat. Did they make a raft?

After did and didn’t, never use the past form. Did they took… is wrong.

Room 3 · The barber shop

June 1962

In June 1962 three men left Alcatraz: Frank Morris and the brothers John and Clarence Anglin. A fourth man, Allen West, stayed behind.

They made false heads from paper, paint and real hair, and put them in their beds.

They took more than fifty raincoats and made a rubber boat. Then they climbed to the roof and went down to the water.

The FBI looked for them for seventeen years and closed the case in 1979. Nobody found them.

West did not open his vent in time. He stayed in his cell, and afterwards he told the investigators how the plan worked.

Room 4 · The workshop

can, can’t, must, mustn’t

can = it is possible. can’t = it is not possible.

You can hear the guard on the stairs. You can’t swim in this water for long — it is too cold.

must = it is necessary. mustn’t = it is forbidden.

You must be quiet. You mustn’t use a light.

After all four, use the base verb with no to. You must to go is wrong.

Room 5 · The water

colder, stronger, more dangerous

Short adjectives take -er and then than.

cold → colder · strong → stronger · safe → safer · big → bigger

Long adjectives take more in front, and than after.

The north shore is more dangerous than the east shore.

Two irregular ones you need tonight: good → better, bad → worse.

Room 5 · The water

A plan you have already made

For a plan you have already decided, use be going to and then the base verb.

I’m going to take the paddle. We’re going to leave at ten.

The negative and the question change the verb be, and nothing else.

They’re not going to wait. Are you going to swim?

Going to go is correct English, even though it sounds strange the first time you say it.
