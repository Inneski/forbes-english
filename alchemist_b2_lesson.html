The Alchemist

Santiago, the desert, and the past perfect — a B2 reading and grammar lesson on Paulo Coelho’s novel

Before you read

What alchemy actually was

The stated goal

Turn base metal into gold. Find the elixir of life.

Practised across the Islamic world, China, India and Europe for well over a thousand years, by people who were entirely serious.

What it really produced

Distillation, laboratory glassware, the first pure acids.

Newton wrote more on alchemy than on physics. Modern chemistry grew directly out of it.

What the novel does with it

The transformation is the person, not the metal.

Santiago is not trying to make gold. He is the material being worked on.

Before you read

Who is who

Santiago

A shepherd from Andalusia, in southern Spain.

He is never named in some translations — he is simply “the boy”.

The old man · the crystal merchant

The one who sends him. The one who slows him down.

The old man claims to be Melchizedek, a king. The merchant is the counter-example: a man who has a dream and will not go.

The Englishman · Fatima · the alchemist

The scholar, the woman at the oasis, the teacher.

The Englishman studies alchemy in books; the alchemist practises it. The book has a view about which of the two learns anything.

Before you read

Two terms the book invents

Personal Legend

The one thing a person is here to do.

Capitalised throughout, as a proper noun. In the Portuguese original it is Lenda Pessoal.

The Soul of the World

The single thing everything is made of and speaks to.

This is the book’s metaphysics in one phrase, and it is what the alchemist teaches Santiago to listen to.

Omens

Signs that point the way, if you are reading.

The first vocabulary word in this lesson, and the mechanism the entire plot runs on.

The story

The story so far — 1 of 5

Santiago is a young shepherd from Andalusia, content with a simple life and the freedom to travel with his flock. Then a dream repeats itself: there is treasure waiting for him near the Egyptian pyramids. He consults a fortune-teller, and later meets an old man who claims to be a king in disguise.

Note content with — satisfied, not eager for more. It is the state the rest of the story disturbs.

The story

The story so far — 2 of 5

The old man persuades him to sell his flock and go in search of his “Personal Legend” — the particular purpose the novel says each person is here to fulfil. Santiago sells the sheep, crosses to North Africa, and is robbed of everything within a day of arriving.

He sells the flock — he does not abandon it. The difference matters: this is a deliberate trade, not a walking away.

The story

The story so far — 3 of 5

Starting again from nothing, he works for a crystal merchant and learns patience. He joins a caravan crossing the desert to an oasis. On the way he meets an Englishman obsessed with alchemy, falls in love with a woman named Fatima, and is tested by hardship, by war between rival tribes, and by his own doubts.

Four of the vocabulary words are in this paragraph: merchant, caravan, oasis, hardship.

The story

The story so far — 4 of 5

At the oasis he meets an alchemist, who teaches him to read the desert, the wind and the sun, and to trust what the novel calls the “Soul of the World”. Through a series of trials Santiago learns that the treasure he has been chasing was never only a thing buried in the ground.

Trial here is not a courtroom. It is a test of what someone can bear — the older sense of the word.

The story

The story so far — 5 of 5

And the ending, because it changes how you read everything before it. Santiago reaches the pyramids and digs, and finds nothing. Robbers beat him, and one of them mentions his own recurring dream: treasure buried under a tree at a ruined church in Spain. It is the exact place Santiago set out from. He goes back, digs, and the gold is there.

So the treasure is real and the journey was the point. A reading that keeps only one of those two is thinner than the book.

Vocabulary

Ten words (1 of 3)

omen

a sign believed to predict what is coming

A dark cloud on the morning of a wedding is a bad omen. The adjective almost always comes with it: good, bad, ill.

flock

a group of sheep or goats

Birds come in a flock too. Cattle come in a herd, fish in a shoal.

caravan

a group of travellers crossing a desert together

In British English a caravan is also the thing you tow behind a car. Context separates them completely.

oasis

a fertile place with water in a desert

Used figuratively as well: an oasis of calm. The plural is oases.

Vocabulary

Ten words (2 of 3)

pursue

to follow or chase something with determination

You pursue a dream, a career, a qualification. Formal; the everyday word is chase or go after.

merchant

someone who buys and sells goods for profit

Slightly old-fashioned or specialised now: a wine merchant, a merchant ship.

destined

certain to happen, as if it were already decided

Destined to + verb, or destined for + noun. It is the adjective at the centre of this novel’s argument.

hardship

severe difficulty or suffering

Uncountable, usually: years of hardship. Not the same as hardness.

Vocabulary

Ten words (3 of 3)

trial

a difficult test of what somebody can bear

The courtroom sense is the commoner one today, so watch the context: the trials of the journey is this sense.

wisdom

good judgement that comes from experience

Uncountable. Not the same as intelligence or knowledge — wisdom is what you do with them.

Grammar · narrative past

The past perfect: had + past participle

What it does

Marks the earlier of two past events.

He sold the sheep because he had decided to go. Deciding first, selling second.

The form

had + past participle — for every person

No has, no agreement, no exceptions. Negative: hadn’t told.

Adverb placement

had already left · had never seen

Between the auxiliary and the participle. Already had left is the commonest slip.

Grammar · narrative past

When you do NOT need it

When the order is already clear

He read the books before he met Santiago.

Before and after do the ordering themselves, so the past perfect becomes optional here. Both versions of that sentence are correct English.

When events are told in order

He sold the sheep, travelled to Africa and was robbed.

Three past simples. Reaching for had here says these happened before some other past moment — and there isn’t one.

The test

Am I stepping back from where the story is?

If yes, use it. If you are just moving forwards, past simple. Overusing the past perfect is as wrong as missing it, and much more common at B2.
