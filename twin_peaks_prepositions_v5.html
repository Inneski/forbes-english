Between Two Worlds

What a preposition does to a sentence — not what it means

C1 · Advanced Prepositions 50 slides

Advanced Theory

Stage 1 · form and function
Advanced theory

The hinge, not the joint

A preposition is not a small joining word. It is the hinge between a noun phrase and the job that phrase does in the sentence.

in + the Black Lodge → in the Black Lodge. The object is always a noun, a pronoun or an -ing form — never a finite verb.

Advanced theory

Three jobs, and only three

1 · Adverbial

Modifies a verb, an adjective, or the whole sentence. Answers where, when, how, why.

Cooper sat in the diner.

2 · Adjectival

Modifies a noun. Answers which one? Usually called a post-modifier.

The woman in the red room spoke backwards.

3 · Nominal

Acts as a noun itself — subject or object. Rare, and formal.

Between the trees is where he vanished.

Advanced theory

Which one?

Inside a noun phrase, a prepositional phrase follows the noun it modifies and does exactly one job: it tells you which one.

The owls [in the forest] are not what they seem. — which owls? Remove the bracket and the sentence stops identifying anything.

Advanced theory

Adverbials · where, when, where to

Place — where?

The position of the action. The commonest and the least noticed.

Cooper sat at the Double R.

Time — when? how long?

Locates the action on a timeline, or measures it.

She vanished before sunrise.

Direction — where to, where from?

Movement with a goal or a source. Contrast this with Place: direction needs a verb of motion.

He walked into the Red Room.

Advanced theory

Adverbials · how, why, despite what

Manner — how?

The way the action is done. Often replaces an adverb.

He spoke in a low whisper.

Reason — why? what for?

The cause or the purpose behind the action.

She went back for the ring.

Concession — despite what?

Something that should have stopped the action and did not.

Despite the danger, he entered.

Advanced theory

The rule that was never a rule

A preposition can sit at the end of a clause — in questions and in relative clauses. This is called stranding, and it is ordinary, correct English.

The lodge he came from. Who did she talk to? Only very formal academic writing prefers the lodge from which he came. Never avoid it in speech.

Multi-word Forms

Stage 2 · compound and complex
Multi-word forms

Two words, one preposition

except for — excluding

Removes one thing from an otherwise complete set.

Except for the owls, the forest was silent.

due to — because of

States a cause. The most formal of the four.

Due to the fog, the road was invisible.

along with — together with

Adds a second item without making it a second subject.

Along with the ring, she left a warning.

apart from — besides / except

Two opposite meanings, and only context separates them.

Apart from Cooper, no one knew the truth.

Multi-word forms

Three words, one preposition

in front of

Directly before, in space. Not before, which is time.

He stood in front of the curtain.

in spite of

Concession. Identical in meaning to despite — and note that despite of does not exist.

In spite of the warning, she entered.

on behalf of

Representing someone, speaking in their place.

He spoke on behalf of the dead.

on top of

Literally above, or figuratively in addition to.

On top of everything, the lights went out.

Multi-word forms

Three words, formal register

by means of

The instrument or method. A formal using.

She communicated by means of the log.

with regard to

Introduces a topic. Common in written business and academic English.

With regard to the case, nothing added up.

as a result of

Consequence. Points backwards to the cause.

As a result of the vision, he changed course.

at the expense of

Something gained, and the price paid for it.

He solved the case at the expense of his sanity.

Multi-word forms

Due to and because of

Both give a cause. Due to is more formal and traditionally follows a form of be — the delay was due to fog.

In modern English they are often interchangeable, but do not open a sentence with Due to… in academic writing. Use Because of… there.

Multi-word forms

The of pattern

in the absence of

A formal without. Common in reports and legal English.

In the absence of evidence, the case was closed.

by virtue of

Gives the reason something is permitted or true.

By virtue of his badge, Cooper was allowed through.

in the wake of

Immediately following an event, usually a bad one.

In the wake of the tragedy, the town fell silent.

Lexical Patterns

Stage 3 · fixed collocations
Lexical patterns

Verbs that demand a preposition

depend on

Rely on. Never depend of.

The truth depends on who is asking.

insist on

Demand firmly. Takes -ing, not an infinitive.

She insisted on seeing the body.

consist of

Be made up of. No passive — never is consisted of.

The Lodge consists of endless corridors.

specialise in

Focus professionally on one area.

She specialised in unsolved disappearances.

Lexical patterns

Result in and result from

The direction of the arrow is the whole difference. The event results in the consequence. The consequence results from the event.

The investigation resulted in an arrest (cause → effect). The arrest resulted from the investigation (effect ← cause).

Lexical patterns

More fixed verb pairs

respond to

React to. The noun is reaction to, never reaction on.

He did not respond to the knock.

apologise for

Express regret for the thing; apologise to the person.

She apologised for the delay.

suffer from

Be affected by an illness or a lasting problem.

The sheriff suffered from nightmares.

believe in

Have faith in — or think a thing exists. Believe alone means only "accept as true".

Cooper believed in the power of dreams.

Lexical patterns

Adjectives that demand a preposition

afraid of / aware of

Both take of. Aware never takes about.

He was aware of being watched.

capable of

Followed by -ing, never by an infinitive.

BOB was capable of anything.

responsible for

The thing, or the person you answer to — both take for.

Who was responsible for the killing?

guilty of / innocent of

Both take of before the crime. Guilty for is wrong.

He was found guilty of deception.

Lexical patterns

Interest, habit and fatigue

interested in

Never interested about — the commonest single error here.

She was intensely interested in the past.

familiar with

You are familiar with a thing; a thing is familiar to you.

Are you familiar with the Black Lodge?

obsessed with

Takes with, not by, when it describes a person.

He was obsessed with the case for years.

tired of

Tired of = bored by. Tired from = exhausted by effort.

She was tired of the lies.

Lexical patterns

Nouns that demand a preposition

effect on

An effect is always on something, never to.

The vision had a lasting effect on him.

solution to / reason for

Two of the most-missed pairs at C1. Not solution of.

There was no obvious solution to the case.

increase in

The thing that grows takes in; the amount takes of.

An increase in strange events was reported.

connection between

Between two things; connection with one thing.

The connection between the two crimes was clear.

The Double Agents

Stage 4 · preposition or conjunction
The double agents

One word, two grammars

Several words work as both. What follows them decides which. Preposition + noun phrase — no subject, no verb. Conjunction + clause — subject and verb.

She left before dawn (preposition). She left before the owl appeared (conjunction). Same word, different grammar.

The double agents

Before, after, until

before / after

Both work either way. Test what follows: a noun, or a subject and verb?

He arrived after midnight. / He arrived after the lights went out.

until

Also both. Note it marks the end of a period, not a point in it.

They waited until nightfall. / …until the fire truck appeared.

since

Both, and it carries a third meaning as well — see the next slide.

Silent since Tuesday. / Silent since she saw the Red Room.

The double agents

The two that will not swap

despite — preposition only

It cannot take a clause. For a clause use although or even though. Despite of is not English.

Despite the danger, he went in. / Although it was dangerous, he went in.

except — needs help

A preposition on its own; it only becomes a conjunction with that after it.

Everyone stayed, except Cooper. / …except that Cooper left a note.

The double agents

The tricky since

Since does three jobs. Preposition of time, conjunction of time, and conjunction of reason — where it simply means because.

Since last winter (preposition). Since Laura died (time). Since you know the truth, you must know the danger (reason). Context decides, and sometimes nothing does.

The double agents

Particle or preposition?

A particle belongs to the verb and changes its meaning. A preposition belongs to the noun phrase after it.

She looked up the case file — particle, = researched. She looked up the hill — preposition, and you can ask "up where?" Only the particle can move: looked it up.
