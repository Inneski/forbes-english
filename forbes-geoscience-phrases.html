Language focus · Rock, trap and seal

Porosity: the matrix and the fractures

matrix porosity

The intergranular pore space of the rock itself. It usually holds most of the fluid in the reservoir, but it gives that fluid up slowly.

fracture porosity

The void space of a fracture network. It holds very little, but it drains quickly, so the fractures are the main conduits to the wellbore.

A reservoir with both is said to exhibit dual porosity behaviour: matrix storage feeding fracture flow. Modelling the two as one system gives the wrong production forecast.

Language focus · Rock, trap and seal

Two ways a trap is made

structural trapping

Deformation makes the closure: a fold — an anticline — or a fault sealing across the reservoir. The shape of the structure holds the hydrocarbons in.

stratigraphic trapping

A change in the rock makes the closure: a sandstone pinching out, a facies change, an unconformity cutting across the bed. No deformation is needed.

Stratigraphic traps are usually subtler on seismic than structural ones, which is why a review panel will ask whether one has been ruled out.

Language focus · Rock, trap and seal

The seal, and how it fails

What seals a trap

The cap rock above the reservoir — shale, evaporite or tight carbonate. Its pore throats are fine enough to hold back a buoyant hydrocarbon column.

How a seal fails

A fault offsets it, fractures open a path through it, or the column grows tall enough to force fluid through the pore throats. That last one is capillary leakage.

Seal integrity is the cap rock’s capacity to keep the column in the trap. Uncertainty about it is one of the commonest reasons a prospect is risked down.

Language focus · Source rock and logs

Source rock: immature, oil window, overmature

Immature

Buried too shallow, or too cool for too long. The kerogen has not yet generated hydrocarbons.

The oil window

Typically about 60–120 °C. The rock generates liquid hydrocarbons and expels them into a carrier bed. A mature source rock has reached this stage.

Overmature

The kerogen has exhausted its oil potential, and oil generated earlier has been cracked to gas.

Maturity follows burial depth and temperature history, not age alone. Cracking is the mechanism that turns oil into gas — not a separate way of losing it.

Language focus · Source rock and logs

Water saturation and what the logs see

water saturation (Sw)

The fraction of the pore space filled with water. What is left is filled with hydrocarbons, so a low Sw is what the interpreter hopes to see.

Why resistivity is read beside it

Saline formation water conducts electricity; oil, gas and tight clean rock do not. High resistivity is therefore usually read as hydrocarbons in the pores.

Anomalously high water saturation means a share of water larger than the other measurements would lead you to expect. It is a flag for the interpreter, not a result.

Language focus · Source rock and logs

When high resistivity does not mean pay

Wet, and still resistive

Fresh, low-salinity formation water is a poor conductor, so a fully wet interval can log as resistive. Very low porosity does the same, and so does deep invasion by resistive mud filtrate.

The opposite anomaly

Conductive minerals — clay, pyrite — pull resistivity down. They produce low-resistivity pay: a good zone that logs as wet, with its Sw overestimated.

Conductive minerals therefore cannot explain an interval that is both wet and highly resistive: they cause the reverse. Fresh formation water is the textbook explanation, with very low porosity and invasion next.

Language focus · Source rock and logs

Cross-bedding and what it records

What it is

Layers inclined within a larger bed, formed as sediment avalanches down the lee face of a migrating dune or ripple. Well-developed means the sets are clear and measurable.

What it tells you

The dip direction of the foresets gives the palaeocurrent direction; the scale and angle of the sets give the energy of the flow.

Currents build cross-beds in fluvial, aeolian, tidal and deltaic settings alike, so a field note says the structure is consistent with a high-energy river system — never that it proves one.

Language focus · Glossary

Six terms the questions use

four-way closure

the structure dips away from the crest on every side, so nothing escapes laterally

net pay

the thickness of an interval that counts as reservoir once the cut-offs are applied

mud weight

the density of the drilling fluid, set to balance the pressure in the formation

bubble point

the pressure at which gas dissolved in the oil starts to come out of solution

cuttings

the rock chips carried up by the mud and examined for lithology and shows

depth conversion

turning a seismic image measured in travel time into one measured in metres

Two more: subsea — measured from sea level, not from the rig floor — and aquifer support, water moving in from below to hold reservoir pressure up.

Language focus · Structure and pressure

Anticlines, crests and closure

the crest

The highest point of an anticlinal fold. Hydrocarbons migrate upward by buoyancy and collect there first, provided there is a seal above.

closure

The vertical distance from the crest down to the lowest closing contour. With four-way closure the beds dip away on every side, so no fault has to seal.

A structure map is contoured on a horizon and quoted in metres subsea: the crest sits inside the innermost contour, and the surface deepens outwards from it.

Language focus · Structure and pressure

Pore pressure, overpressure and mud weight

The hydrostatic gradient

Normal pore pressure is the weight of the column of formation water above the point of measurement — roughly 10 kPa for every metre of depth.

overpressure

Pore fluid pressure above that normal value, usually where compacting shale could not expel its water. The mud weight has to be raised to balance it.

Too light a mud and the formation flows into the well — a well control incident. Too heavy and the mud fractures the formation and is lost into it.

Language focus · Wellsite to production

Shows in the cuttings, porosity in the log

oil shows

Visible or measurable traces of hydrocarbon in cuttings, core or mud. Residual shows are immobile: they record that migration happened at some stage, not that it is happening now.

effective porosity

The interconnected pore space available to flow, once isolated pores and clay-bound water are excluded. Total porosity counts all of it and reads higher.

The two porosities are easy to confuse in a report. A figure quoted after cut-offs and exclusions is the effective one.

Language focus · Wellsite to production

From travel time to depth

two-way time

Seismic data is recorded in the time a wave takes to travel down to a reflector and back, not in metres. The section is an image in time.

the check-shot survey

A receiver is lowered to known depths in the well and the travel time from surface is measured directly. That calibrates the velocity model used for depth conversion.

Get the velocity model wrong and a mapped crest moves or a closure disappears. Depth conversion is where a good seismic interpretation is most often lost.

Language focus · Wellsite to production

What pushes the oil to the well

solution gas drive

Below the bubble point, gas dissolved in the oil comes out of solution and expands, pushing oil toward the wellbore. Recovery is usually modest.

gas cap drive

A free gas cap above the oil expands as the reservoir is produced and drives the oil down toward the perforations.

water drive

An aquifer moves in as pressure falls, holding the pressure up and sweeping the oil ahead of it. Usually the most efficient of the three.

With limited aquifer support and no gas cap, solution gas is what is left — which is why the recovery factor in that case is expected to be modest.

Language focus · Glossary

Seven terms from the report — maturity, faults, seals

vitrinite reflectance

a measure of thermal maturity, read from the optical reflectivity of vitrinite particles in the source rock and quoted as %Ro

fault throw

the vertical component of the displacement across a fault plane; the horizontal component is the heave

capillary entry pressure

the pressure a non-wetting fluid needs before it can enter the pore throats of the seal and displace the wetting fluid

gas–water contact

the level in the reservoir below which the pore space holds water rather than gas

Note the pair: throw and heave are the two components of one displacement, so quoting the wrong one misstates the offset at reservoir level.

net-to-gross ratio

the proportion of an interval that meets the cut-offs for reservoir quality — net thickness over gross thickness

overpressure

pore fluid pressure that exceeds the hydrostatic pressure expected at that depth

a group of related sedimentary facies that together characterise one depositional environment
