Hedging: how scientists avoid overclaiming

Academic English marks how certain a claim is. A hedge downgrades it — largely, broadly, long assumed, a matter of considerable debate — so the writer states what the evidence supports and no more.

The fossil record is incomplete. → a flat fact.
The fossil record, largely incomplete though it is, paints a vivid picture. → the gap is conceded, and the claim survives it.

Hedges are not vagueness. Long assumed says a belief was held without sufficient evidence — a precise thing to say about a field's history.

Language focus · 2 of 3

The verbs that go with the nouns

At C1 the grammar is rarely the problem — the partner word is. You draw conclusions, a finding carries implications, data is consistent with a theory, a mod harnesses an engine.

The near-misses are the trap. Make conclusions, bring implications and compatible with the data are all grammatical and all wrong — a fluent reader hears them immediately.

Learn collocations as whole chunks, not as verb plus noun assembled fresh each time.

Language focus · 3 of 3

Front the important part

Cleft: It was the density of the armour that protected it.
Negative fronting: Not until CT scanning were researchers able to look inside.

So + adjective: So entrenched had the misconception become that textbooks kept repeating it.
Conditional inversion: Were the hypothesis to prove viable, the ethics would follow.

All four move the emphasis to the front of the sentence, and all four invert subject and auxiliary once something negative or emphatic leads. Complex noun phrases do the same job more quietly: the question of whether non-avian dinosaurs possessed complex social cognition remains contested.
