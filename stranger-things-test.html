Language focus · Describing a picture

How far away is it?

in the foreground

The part of the picture closest to you, right at the front. There is a deer in the foreground.

in the middle

Between the other things, with something on each side. A boy is standing in the middle.

in the background

Furthest away, behind everything else. Tall trees rise in the background.

These three answer one question: how far from you is it? Use them with is or is standing — the deer is in the foreground.

Language focus · Describing a picture

Which side, which corner, who is touching whom

on the right-hand side

One side of the picture rather than the centre. The deer is on the right-hand side.

in the bottom left-hand corner

Where two edges meet. Name them in this order: top or bottom, then left or right.

a hand on her shoulder

For people touching: he has a hand on her shoulder. English says whose shoulder it is.

British English hyphenates right-hand and left-hand. A corner needs two words: top or bottom, then left or right.

Language focus · Words for the scene

An autumn day in the woods

autumn

The season between summer and winter. British English: autumn. American English: the fall.

leaves

One leaf, two leaves. The leaves fell from the trees like orange snow.

a scarf

You wind it round your neck against the cold. One scarf, two scarves.

Both leaf and scarf change f to ves in the plural, like half → halves and shelf → shelves.

Language focus · Words for the scene

Colour and light

rusty

The reddish-brown of old iron left out in the rain. Everything down there had a rusty colour.

bright

Giving out a lot of light. The lab lights were so bright they hurt her eyes. Opposite: dim.

shining through

Light from one side passing all the way to the other. A torch was shining through the tunnel.

Shine is irregular: shine → shone. Light shines through something — through the trees, through a window, through the dark.

Language focus · Words for the scene

Two places the kids go

a bowling alley

You roll a heavy ball down a polished lane to knock over ten pins. Strike, spare, gutter ball.

a rollerdisco

You put on roller skates and go round and round to music. Friday night in Hawkins.

Bowling alley is two words; rollerdisco is usually written as one, though you will also see roller disco.

Language focus · Words for the scene

Three kinds of open land

a plain

A wide area of flat land with no hills. The Upside Down looks like a vast plain.

a desert

So dry that almost nothing grows: sand, bare rock, no water.

a field

A piece of land with a hedge or a fence round it, where crops grow or animals graze.

Flat is not the same as dry. A plain can be green farmland; a desert can have dunes and hills. Plain is also an adjective meaning simple or ordinary — a plain shirt has no pattern.

Language focus · Grammar

has or have

he, she, it → has

The man has a remote control. Eleven has powers. The Demogorgon has no face.

I, you, we, they → have

I have a torch. We have a plan. Mike and Dustin have bikes.

A single name is he, she or it: Eleven has. Two names joined by and are they: Mike and Dustin have.

Language focus · Grammar

Two traps

There is no form haves

The -s is already inside has. He haves does not exist in English at all.

has got and have got

British English often adds got: he has got a torch means the same as he has a torch, and the -s stays where it was.

In questions and negatives the little verb takes the -s, and have goes back to its plain form: Does she have a torch? — never Does she has.

Language focus · Grammar

Where nor really belongs

neither … nor

The pair always travels together. Will is neither in Hawkins nor in the Upside Down.

nor + inversion

Opening a second negative clause. She does not watch television, nor does she listen to the radio.

Watch the word order after nor: the verb jumps in front of the subject. Nor does she listen, not nor she does listen.

Language focus · Grammar

— and where or is right

One verb, two things → or

I do not like the Upside Down or the Demogorgon. One verb, two things after it — and this is correct English.

Give nor a verb of its own

I like neither the Upside Down nor the Demogorgon, or I do not like the Upside Down, nor do I like the Demogorgon.

So or after a negative is good English. What is wrong is neither … or, and nor she does.

Language focus · Grammar

Happening now, or simply true?

Happening right now → be + -ing

The two agents are shaking hands. Eleven is closing the gate.

States → present simple

look, seem, know, like, want, believe, own. The agents look very serious. Eleven knows where the gate is.

A few verbs do both, with two meanings: they look serious (= they seem) but they are looking at the map (= an action in progress).

Language focus · Grammar

there is and there are

there is + one thing

There is a torch on the table. There is a deer in the foreground.

there are + more than one

There are a lot of trees here. There are two people in the picture.

The verb agrees with what comes after it, not with there. And there are introduces something new, while they are points back at something already mentioned.
