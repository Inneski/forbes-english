The First Two Paragraphs

The introduction and the overview — the two paragraphs that decide your Task Achievement band, written before you describe a single figure.

C1 Advanced Task 1 & Task 2 openings 17 slides
The shape

Two paragraphs, before any detail

A Task 1 report has four paragraphs. The first two come before any detail: an introduction saying what the chart shows, and an overview saying what it mainly shows.

Write them first and the body has somewhere to go. Write them last and they turn into a summary of what you have already said — which is why so many reports repeat themselves.

The overview is the one paragraph you cannot leave out. A report without one is capped on Task Achievement, however good the rest of it is.

Move one

The introduction is a paraphrase

Take the prompt and change the nouns and the verb, reordering if it helps. One sentence is enough. Two is the most this paragraph is worth.

Leave alone: the figures, the units, the dates and any proper nouns. Changing 1990 into 1991, or a percentage into a number, is not paraphrase — it is an error.

Words lifted straight from the prompt are not counted toward your 150. Copy the whole first line and you can be twenty words short without knowing it.

Move two

The overview names the big picture

Two or three sentences on what a reader would notice from across the room: the highest and the lowest, the overall direction, the widest gap, the point where something turns.

No specific figures. Figures are the body’s job. An overview full of numbers is a body paragraph that has wandered up to the top of the page.

It may sit straight after the introduction or right at the end. Both are accepted. Straight after is safer — you cannot then run out of time and lose it.

Question 1

Which one is a paraphrase?

The prompt reads: The chart shows the percentage of households in three countries that owned a car between 1990 and 2010.

Question 2

Which one is doing an overview’s work?

All four sentences are true of the same chart. Only one of them belongs in the overview.

What an overview can say

Four moves — pick two

Highest and lowest. Which category leads and which trails, across the whole period.

Overall direction. Up, down, flat — or one of each, if they differ.

The widest gap. Where the distance between categories is greatest.

The turning point. Where a line changes direction, or two lines cross.

Two of these are enough for an overview at 150 words. Use all four and it becomes a body paragraph in disguise.

🗣Discussion · in pairs

Describing a chart you cannot see is the fastest test of whether an overview is doing its job.

  • Describe a chart to your partner without showing it and without one figure. They sketch what they think it looks like. Compare, then swap.
  • Take thirty seconds and any chart from the news. Give the introduction and the overview aloud, nothing else. Your partner times you.
  • One of you argues the overview belongs second, the other that it belongs last. Argue it on what each costs you in the exam room.
