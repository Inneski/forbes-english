Why do high achievers feel like frauds? — advanced reading, academic style and a cultural comparison

Before you read

Three things to hold in mind

The claim

Accomplishment does not settle the question of whether you deserved it.

Decide now whether you believe that, and see whether the text changes your mind.

The cultural question

Kenson (謙遜) — modesty as a social virtue.

Does a culture that expects public modesty make private doubt easier to voice, or harder? There is no single right answer, and the text does not give one.

The text

Reading — paragraph 1 of 8

Even after publishing book after book and winning several of the most prestigious awards in American letters, Maya Angelou could not escape the nagging doubt that she had not really earned her accomplishments. Albert Einstein described himself in similar terms — as an "involuntary swindler" whose work did not deserve the attention it received.

nagging: persistently troubling, refusing to go away. involuntary: not by choice; a swindler deceives people for gain.

The text

Reading — paragraph 2 of 8

Accomplishments at that level are rare, but the feeling of fraudulence is extremely common. Why can so many of us not shake the feeling that we have not earned what we have, or that our ideas are not worth other people's attention?

shake a feeling: to rid yourself of an unwanted emotion. Compare shake off a cold. A very common collocation and an easy one to miss.

The text

Reading — paragraph 3 of 8

The psychologist Pauline Rose Clance was the first to study this unwarranted sense of insecurity. Working as a therapist, she noticed that many of her undergraduate patients shared one concern: despite high grades, they did not believe they deserved their places at the university. Some believed their acceptance had been an admissions error.

admissions error: a mistake in the enrolment process. The students feared their acceptance was accidental rather than earned.

The text

Reading — paragraph 4 of 8

With her colleague Suzanne Imes, Clance first studied the impostor phenomenon in female college students and faculty, establishing pervasive feelings of fraudulence in that group. It has since been found across gender, race, age and a wide range of occupations, though it may be more prevalent among, and disproportionately affect, underrepresented or disadvantaged groups.

Read the hedging in the last clause: may be, not is. Step 5 comes back to this sentence — it is the model for the whole grammar section.

The text

Reading — paragraph 5 of 8

To call it a syndrome is to downplay how universal it is. It is not a disease or an abnormality, though research does find it associated with anxiety and low mood. People who are highly skilled tend to assume everyone else is equally skilled, and that assumption can spiral into a feeling that they do not deserve the accolades and opportunities they receive.

To call X is to Y is a concessive pattern worth stealing. Note also that the original of this passage said impostorism "isn't necessarily tied to depression or anxiety" — later work (Bravata et al., 2020) reports consistent associations, so the claim is softened here.

The text

Reading — paragraph 6 of 8

Everyone is susceptible to pluralistic ignorance: we each doubt ourselves privately but believe we are alone in doing so, because no one else says it out loud. Since it is hard to know how hard our peers work, how difficult they find a task, or how much they doubt themselves, there is no easy way to dismiss the feeling that we are the least capable person in the room.

This paragraph is the answer to one of the comprehension questions. Read it twice.

The text

Reading — paragraph 7 of 8

Intense feelings of this kind stop people sharing good ideas, or applying for jobs and programmes where they would do well. The text's own strongest claim is that the most surefire way to fight impostor feelings is to talk about them. Even hearing that a mentor has felt the same thing can help; so, sometimes, can simply learning that there is a name for it.

surefire is informal and unusually strong. Notice how far it sits from the careful may be of paragraph 4 — the same author, two very different levels of commitment.

The text

Reading — paragraph 8 of 8

Once you are aware of the phenomenon, you can work against it by collecting and revisiting positive feedback about yourself. We may never be able to banish these feelings entirely, but we can have open conversations about academic and professional difficulty — and be franker about some simple truths: you have talent, you are capable, and you belong.

Academic style

Nominalisation: turn the verb into a noun

The move

They found that people felt fraudulent. → Their finding of pervasive fraudulence.

The verb becomes the head noun and the clause collapses into a noun phrase.

Why it is done

Denser, more formal, and impersonal.

It lets you drop the agent. Their acceptance does not say who accepted them.

The cost

Overdone, it becomes unreadable.

Two nominalisations in a sentence is style; four is fog. C1 writing is knowing where to stop.

Academic style

Hedging: claiming exactly as much as the evidence allows

The devices

may · might · appears to · tends to · suggests

Modal verbs, reporting verbs, and adverbs of frequency all do this work.

What it is not

It is not politeness, and it is not weakness.

This is where it differs from kenson (謙遜). Kenson is about the speaker; hedging is about the evidence.

The test

Would a replication failure embarrass this sentence?

If yes, hedge it. Paragraph 7 of the reading fails that test — surefire is the one place the text overcommits.

Academic style

Collocation: the verb that belongs with the noun

Why it matters at C1

The grammar can be perfect and the phrase still wrong.

Have self-doubt is not an error. Harbour self-doubt is what a reader of academic English expects.

How to learn them

Store the pair, never the single word.

Learn garner an accolade, not garner. The vocabulary cards in this lesson give the collocates for exactly this reason.

Register travels with them

garner · harbour · perpetuate · entertain

All four are formal. Using one in casual speech is as marked as using get in a paper.

Before the error correction

Five mistakes, and why each one happens

despite / in spite of

One takes of; the other never does.

The two get crossed, and despite of is the result. There is no such phrase.

affect / effect · -ly on adjectives

affect = verb · effect = noun

And an adjective after is stays an adjective: is more prevalent, not is more prevalently.

that / which · parallel gerunds

the feeling that… · better than keeping quiet

After feeling, idea, fact and belief, the clause takes that. And both sides of than must take the same form.

Error correction · 1 / 2

Write the correction

One word each. Only the wrong word changes.

Wrong: Despite of her achievements, she doubted herself.  →  her achievements, she doubted herself.

Wrong: It may effects experienced professionals.  →  It may experienced professionals.

Wrong: The phenomenon is more prevalently in some groups.  →  The phenomenon is more in some groups.

Error correction · 2 / 2

Write the correction

Wrong: She could not shake the feeling which she had not deserved it.  →  …the feeling she had not deserved it.

Wrong: Talking about it is better than keep quiet.  →  …better than quiet.

Cultural connections

謙遜 (kenson) and impostor feelings

Kenson is public

Downplaying your ability out loud is polite.

It is a social performance, and everyone in the room knows it is one.

Impostor feeling is private

It is a belief the person wishes they did not hold.

The difference is whether you would be relieved to stop.

The open question

Does a script for public modesty help or hide?

It may make the words available — or it may make the real thing impossible to distinguish from the performance.

Cultural connections

空気を読む and pluralistic ignorance

The same mechanism

Both involve reading what others appear to feel.

And both involve getting it wrong, in the same direction, at the same time as everyone else.

Where they differ

One is a skill; the other is an error.

Reading the room is something you can be good at. Pluralistic ignorance is what happens when everyone reads it correctly and it is still wrong.

Why the text cares

It is the reason talking about it works.

One person saying it out loud breaks the ignorance for everyone in earshot.

Cultural connections

受験 (juken) and the achievement paradox

The setup

Years of preparation for one selection.

Entry is framed as the culmination of the effort — the point at which the question is settled.

What happens inside

Everyone in the room passed the same exam.

Paragraph 5: highly skilled people assume everyone else is equally skilled. A selective institution makes that assumption locally true.

The paradox

The harder the selection, the less it settles.

This is a good example for the writing task: universal psychology, culturally specific trigger.
