A · Nominalisation 名詞化

B · Hedging Language 断定を避ける表現
From the text
"…it may be more prevalent and disproportionately affect the experiences of underrepresented groups."

"We may never be able to banish these feelings entirely…"
Hedging signals intellectual caution — the writer acknowledges uncertainty rather than overstating. Contrasted with Japanese direct modesty (謙遜), English academic hedging functions structurally, not personally.

Cultural Connections
日本文化との比較　— 文化的視点から考える
Explore how concepts in the text connect to — and diverge from — Japanese cultural frameworks. Use this section to deepen your analytical writing.
Key Concept
謙遜 (Kenson) vs. Impostor Syndrome
Kenson is a culturally valued form of modesty in Japan — publicly downplaying one's abilities is considered polite and socially appropriate. Impostor syndrome, by contrast, is an internal, private experience of fraudulence that the sufferer wishes they did not feel. Consider: does a culture of public humility make internal doubt harder to voice, or does it provide a social script that normalises it?
Key Concept
空気を読む (Kuuki wo yomu) & Pluralistic Ignorance
The Japanese concept of kuuki wo yomu — reading the atmosphere, understanding unspoken group norms — maps closely onto pluralistic ignorance described in the text. In both cases, people monitor what others appear to feel and adjust their expressed beliefs accordingly — often misjudging what others truly experience.
Key Concept
高学歴競争とイメージのギャップ
Japan's highly competitive university entrance system (juken 受験) creates enormous pressure to reach certain institutions. Students who succeed may feel — like Clance's patients — that their success was accidental. Yet the very intensity of the selection process makes this belief especially irrational. This is a rich example of how cultural context shapes the triggers of impostorism even when the psychology is universal.
