## 1. The Creature's size and face change from scene to scene

This is the defect that makes the game unusable in a classroom. In one scene
he is a large man kneeling at a table; two scenes later he is a figure the
height of an ice cliff, and in one he has a different face altogether. It
reads as two or three different characters.

### The character sheet — this is the Creature, in every picture

Take it from `22_knock.webp` and `25_demand.webp`, which are right:

> **Head:** a flat-topped blocky head, wider at the crown. **Skin:** olive
> green, blockier and rougher than the human characters. **Eyes:** amber /
> orange, lit from within. **Face:** a small cross-shaped stitch on the
> forehead, more on the shoulders. **Hair:** cropped and dark, barely there —
> **never long, never shoulder-length, never loose.** **Clothes:** a ragged
> dark layered coat, too small for him, hanging in strips at the cuffs.

### The height rule

> The Creature is **eight feet tall (2.4 m)** — Shelley's own figure. That is
> about **1.35× a tall man**: head and shoulders above Victor, no more. He is
> broad, heavy and wrongly proportioned, and that is where the menace comes
> from. He is **never** taller than a doorway, a cottage roof, a mast, an ice
> cliff or a ship.

Two anchors, since almost every scene contains one:

- **Beside a person** — the top of his head reaches that person's height plus
  about one head. Look at `22_knock.webp`: kneeling, he is level with a
  seated old man. That is correct.
- **Alone in a landscape** — size him against something of known height in
  the same frame: a doorway, a sled, a boat, a man. In a wide Arctic or
  Alpine shot he is a **small** figure the eye has to find.
  **`final_alps_pursuit.webp` is the reference for this** — Victor with a
  lantern in the foreground, the Creature a tiny silhouette on a distant
  ridge. Every wide shot should read like that one.

Distance makes him small. It does not make him big.

## 2. The wrong answers do not test the grammar

Every question targets *going to*, which is right and should stay. The
problem is the three options. In 21 of the 44 questions they look like this:

```
['is going to', 'are going to', 'is going leaving']
```

Option 2 is a subject-agreement slip. Option 3 is not English. So the learner
never decides **which tense the sentence needs** — only which form of `be`
goes with the subject. Three questions in, the strategy is "match is/are to
the subject, reject the one without *to*", and the whole game falls without
reading a story panel. The badge above the question already names the tense,
so the answer is given away twice.

**Fix the distractors, not the prompts.** The prompts are good — the scenes
are written with exactly the visible evidence a *going to* item wants. Offer
the three English futures and let the context decide:

```
Before:  "Sit by the fire. I ___ tell you my story."
         ['am going to', 'is going to', 'am going telling']

After:   "Sit by the fire. I ___ tell you my story."
         ['am going to', 'will probably', 'am telling']
```

Rules for every question:

- All three options are **real English** and **real learner errors**. Never a
  nonsense form like *"is going leaving"*, *"am going telling"* or
  *"don't going to"*.
- The wrong options are **other future forms** — `will`, present continuous,
  present simple — not agreement slips.
- Keep at most **two** agreement-based items in the whole game, as a change
  of pace.
- The correct option is never the longest one.

Send these as a table — scene id, the three new options, which is correct —
not as a rebuilt file.

## 3. Two questions to reword

- `18_alps`'s explanation is *"The speaker says I am going to tell."* That
  restates the answer instead of naming the rule. Every explanation should
  say **why**: *"A decision already made, announced with going to."*
- Any item whose explanation ends with a note that another answer is also
  possible is broken. Rewrite the prompt so one form works, or change the
  distractor. Do not warn a learner off a correct answer.

## 4. The Creature is "he", never "it" and never "they"

He is a person in this telling. Check every English string. The export had
*"The Creature Opens Its Eyes"* as a scene title; that kind of thing.

In German and Spanish, *die Kreatur* and *la Criatura* force feminine
agreement — leave grammatical gender alone rather than mangling the sentence.
English has no excuse.

## 5. What is already right — please do not change it

- **One grammar point, all the way through.** The game is *going to* and

  target.
- **`21_approach_choice`** — talk to De Lacey, or leave firewood. Two honest
  routes with different consequences, rejoining cleanly. That is the model
  for a route choice in this world.
- The palette, the voxel style, the aurora, and the Creature's design as it
  appears in `22_knock` and `25_demand`.
- `final_alps_pursuit.webp`, which is the correct way to shoot him at
  distance.

## 6. Before you deliver

2. Every picture: flat-topped green head, amber eyes, cropped dark hair,

4. New option sets: all real English, wrong options are other future forms,

5. Report the list of files changed and the largest file size.

| Where | Victor |
