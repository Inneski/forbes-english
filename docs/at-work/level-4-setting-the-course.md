# Book 4 — Setting the Course (B2/C1)

*Strategising and leading.* Read `README.md` first: the world, the cast, the
strands, the grading and the artwork spec all live there. This file is the
fifteen units.

**The company.** Arden Health, Berlin. Nine hundred people in six countries.
A video-consultation platform with its own clinicians, home-testing kits with
a hardware supplier behind them, and a Series C round in the bank. Hybrid by
design, data-heavy by nature, and expanding across Europe faster than its
plumbing. Larkline, the Copenhagen e-bike maker, offers Arden to its staff.

**The year.** Grace Mbeki arrives from Johannesburg as Director of Operations
in Unit 1, to lead two hundred people who have been running as six teams.
She inherits a supplier under pressure, a promotion process nobody trusts, a
phone line that is too thin for winter, and a small analytics firm halfway
through being bought. By Unit 15 she has renegotiated the supplier, run a
six-hour outage into the press and out again, delivered twelve redundancies
in person, pitched a Series D, and is closing a conference with what she
learned. Nadia is the founder, Felix runs the technology, Ravi runs the
money, and Beatriz — who came from Larkline last year — runs the people.

**Grading.** B2 into C1. Dialogues 200–280 words, 12–16 lines, complex
sentences, hedged where a leader would hedge. Everything is available — third
and mixed conditionals, inversion, advanced passives, participle clauses —
and each unit foregrounds its own point. See README §5.

**Artwork accent:** cobalt. Grace's cobalt coat is in every picture she is
in. Stem and slots: README §7.

## Unit 1 · The Decentralised Team
*Six teams that happen to share a logo.*

**Can do:** set expectations for a hybrid team on your first day in charge; use continuous forms for what is temporary, developing or deliberately unusual, including with verbs that are usually stative; hedge and soften an opinion without losing it.

**Scenario.** Grace's first leads meeting. Two hundred people in six countries, running as six teams. She sets three expectations — no approvals for small things, no micromanagement from anyone, a listener in someone else's stand-up every week — and invites the room to tell her where she is wrong. Felix does, and then changes his mind.

| strand | |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | silo, cohesion, autonomy, micromanagement, approval, overlap, stand-up, distributed, alignment, expectation, accountability · operate as, rely on, learn to, be present in · *how shall I put it* |
| Grammar | continuous forms for what is temporary or developing (*we're operating as six teams*), and the dynamic use of normally stative verbs when the speaker means a process rather than a state: *I'm hearing that …, I'm noticing one lead, she's being very present, I'm having second thoughts, I'm relying on someone* |
| Communication | leading a hybrid team and setting expectations |
| In the Room | hedging and softening opinions — saying a hard thing at the right temperature |

**Lead-in.** How many of the people you work with have you met in person this year? Look at the picture. Which of the six groups is talking to another?

**Grammar in the dialogue.**
- It seems to me **we're operating** as six teams that happen to share a logo.
- **I'm hearing** that they talk to each other, not to operations.
- **I'm noticing** one lead who **is being** — how shall I put it — very present in her team's inboxes.
- **I'm having** second thoughts about my "harsh" remark. — Keep them. **I'm relying** on someone to tell me when I'm wrong.

**In the Room — phrase box.**
- *It seems to me …*
- *That's perhaps a little harsh.*
- *I may be wrong, but …*
- *I'd tend to call that …*
- *To some extent, that's true.*
- *I'm not entirely sure that …*
- *It's possible that …*

**Model dialogue.** *Grace's first leads meeting. Felix, Beatriz and Ravi.*

GRACE: Let me set out how I'd like this to work, and then tell me where I'm wrong. Two hundred people, six countries, and *it seems to me* **we're operating** as six teams that happen to share a logo.
FELIX: *That's perhaps a little harsh.* The engineering teams **talk** every day.
GRACE: *I may be wrong, but* **I'm hearing** that they talk to each other, not to operations. *I'd tend to call* that a silo, even a friendly one.
BEATRIZ: *To some extent that's true.* I **saw** the same thing at Larkline. The cure was overlap, not more meetings.
GRACE: Agreed. So, expectations. One: **I'm not going to be** in every call. If a lead needs my approval to move a shift, **we're doing** it wrong.
RAVI: *I'm not entirely sure* the leads want that much autonomy. Some of them **like** being told.
GRACE: Then **I'm asking** them to learn. Two: no micromanagement from me, and none from them. **I'm noticing** one lead who **is being** — *how shall I put it* — very present in her team's inboxes.
BEATRIZ: I think I know who you mean.
GRACE: Three: cohesion. Every team **sends** one person to another country's stand-up each week. Not to report — to listen.
FELIX: That might actually work. **I'm having** second thoughts about my "harsh" remark.
GRACE: Keep them. **I'm relying** on someone to tell me when I'm wrong. *It's possible that* today it was me.

**The case.**
- *Speak:* You have just taken over a team of thirty in three cities. Set three expectations in two minutes, hedging each one exactly enough; your partner, a lead, pushes back on one and you hold it.
- *Write:* Write the message to all two hundred after the meeting: what you're noticing, what you're asking, what you're not going to do — with five continuous forms, two of them dynamic uses of stative verbs.

**Homework.**
- *Do:* Write the message to all two hundred after your first leads meeting.
- *Self-check:*
  1. It seems to me we ___ (operate) as six teams.
  2. I ___ (hear) that they talk to each other, not to us.
  3. Some of the leads ___ (like) being told.
  4. One lead ___ (be) very present in inboxes at the moment.
  5. I ___ (rely) on someone to tell me when I'm wrong.
- *Key:* 1 're operating · 2 'm hearing · 3 like · 4 is being · 5 'm relying

## Unit 2 · AI Augmentation
*It is understood that the clinician signs every note.*

**Can do:** pitch a workflow overhaul to the people who must approve it; use advanced passive structures to report what is thought, expected and estimated without pinning it on a person; anticipate objections and answer them before they are asked.

**Scenario.** Grace and Felix propose that a generative model drafts clinical notes for the clinician to sign. Sixty medical secretaries type those notes today. Nadia's questions are safety and the secretaries; Ravi's is the board's; Grace answers each before it lands, and Nadia approves a two-clinic pilot on one condition.

| strand | |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | generative, large language model, draft, displacement, upskilling, retraining, reviewer, anonymised, hosted, vendor, flag, consultation, volume · sign off, catch (an error), dress up |
| Grammar | advanced passives that report a view without a subject: *It is understood that the clinician signs every note. It is thought that this tool replaces typing. It is expected that volume will double. It has been shown elsewhere that … The draft is flagged where the model wasn't sure.* |
| Communication | pitching a workflow overhaul to stakeholders: benefit, risk, people, mechanism |
| In the Room | anticipating objections — naming the question before it is asked |

**Lead-in.** What part of your job could a machine draft for you to check? Would you want it to? Look at the picture. Who signs?

**Grammar in the dialogue.**
- **It is understood** that the clinician signs every note, and nothing **is sent** unsigned.
- **It is thought** — by them, and they're right — that this tool replaces typing.
- **It is expected** that consultation volume will double next year, and **it is estimated** that each note takes twenty minutes to type.
- **It's been shown** elsewhere that reviewing a draft takes a quarter of the time of typing one.

**In the Room — phrase box.**
- *I'll start with the objection I know is coming.*
- *Which brings me to the part we've thought hardest about.*
- *Let's not dress it up.* / *I'd rather we didn't either.*
- *I can already hear the board asking …*
- *I'd anticipate one more question: …*
- *You've answered the questions I had.*

**Model dialogue.** *Grace and Felix pitch to Nadia and Ravi.*

GRACE: *I'll start with the objection I know is coming.* You're going to ask whether **it is safe** for a model to draft clinical notes. The answer: **it is understood** that the clinician signs every note, and nothing **is sent** unsigned.
NADIA: That was my first question. My second is the secretaries.
FELIX: *Which brings me to the part we've thought hardest about.* Sixty people **are currently employed** to type notes. **It is thought** — by them, and they're right — that this tool replaces typing.
RAVI: So it's displacement. *Let's not dress it up.*
GRACE: *I'd rather we didn't either.* But **it is expected** that consultation volume will double next year, and **it is estimated** that each note takes twenty minutes to type. If nothing **is changed**, we hire sixty more typists.
NADIA: Or?
FELIX: Or the sixty **are retrained** as note reviewers and patient coordinators. **It's been shown** elsewhere that reviewing a draft takes a quarter of the time of typing one.
RAVI: *I can already hear the board asking* what the model **is trained** on.
GRACE: On our own anonymised notes, and **it is hosted** in our own environment. Nothing **is shared** with the vendor. *I'd anticipate one more question:* what happens when it's wrong?
NADIA: Go on.
GRACE: The same thing that happens when a typist is wrong — the clinician catches it. Except **the draft is flagged** where the model wasn't sure, which a typist never does.
NADIA: *You've answered the questions I had.* Pilot with two clinics. And Felix — the sixty **are told** first, by us, in person.
FELIX: Already in the plan.

**The case.**
- *Speak:* Pitch a tool that automates a task your colleagues do by hand. Open with the objection you know is coming, answer three more before they are asked, and report every claim with an advanced passive.
- *Write:* Write the one-page proposal for the pilot: what is understood, what is expected, what has been shown, what is not shared, and what the sixty are offered — in the passive throughout, then one active sentence about who tells them.

**Homework.**
- *Do:* Write the one-page pilot proposal in the passive.
- *Self-check:*
  1. People think this tool replaces typing. → It ___ that this tool replaces typing.
  2. Experts expect volume to double. → It ___ that volume will double.
  3. Studies have shown reviewing is faster. → It ___ that reviewing is faster.
  4. We estimate each note takes twenty minutes. → It ___ that each note takes twenty minutes.
  5. Sixty people currently type notes. → Sixty people ___ to type notes.
- *Key:* 1 is thought · 2 is expected · 3 has been shown · 4 is estimated · 5 are currently employed

- `u02-opener` — a long row of desks with typists in silhouette, the far end of the row dissolving into a single screen with a page of text on it and a pen poised above a signature line; Grace in her cobalt coat and Felix in his hoodie at the screen, Nadia standing, Ravi with his folder; the pen cobalt

## Unit 3 · Stakeholder Capitalism
*Four numbers, none of them revenue.*

**Can do:** present non-financial metrics to an investor's analyst; build and unpack complex noun phrases so that a dense idea carries; clarify and rephrase until both sides mean the same thing.

**Scenario.** An ESG analyst from a Series C investor works through Arden's deck with Grace and Ravi: the share of consultations reaching people with no local doctor, avoided travel emissions that Arden reports but refuses to sell as credits, a board moving slowly from founder control to independence, and clinician turnover in the first two years.

| strand | |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | governance, independent director, carbon credit, avoided emissions, triple bottom line, stakeholder, materiality, reach, retention, turnover, founder-controlled, contested · monetise, resist, report, attribute to |
| Grammar | complex noun phrases — a head noun with its modifiers, prepositional phrases and clauses stacked before and after: *the share of our consultations reaching people the system otherwise misses; a reduction in patient travel emissions attributable to remote consultation; the risk of losing the founder's clinical vision in a rush to governance* |
| Communication | presenting non-financial metrics and ESG to a financial audience |
| In the Room | clarifying and rephrasing complex ideas — the phrases that unpack a noun phrase into a sentence |

**Lead-in.** Name one number your organisation is proud of that isn't money. Look at the picture. Which of the four bars is people?

**Grammar in the dialogue.**
- **The share of our consultations reaching people the system otherwise misses** is the number we care most about.
- **A reduction in patient travel emissions attributable to remote consultation** — forty thousand tonnes last year.
- **The temptation to monetise avoided emissions through carbon credits** is one we've resisted.
- **Staff turnover among clinicians in their first two years** — down from thirty to nineteen per cent.

**In the Room — phrase box.**
- *Put simply: …*
- *In other words, …*
- *To be clear, …*
- *Let me rephrase that back to you: …*
- *That's a fair way to put it.*
- *What that means in practice is …*
- *If I can summarise: …*

**Model dialogue.** *An investor's ESG analyst with Grace and Ravi.*

ANALYST: Talk me through the non-financial metrics. Your deck has "the proportion of consultations delivered to patients in areas with no local practice" at nineteen per cent. What am I looking at?
GRACE: *Put simply:* one in five of our patients has no doctor within thirty minutes. **The share of our consultations reaching people the system otherwise misses** is the number we care most about.
ANALYST: And the carbon line?
RAVI: **A reduction in patient travel emissions attributable to remote consultation** — forty thousand tonnes last year. *In other words*, journeys to a clinic that didn't happen.
ANALYST: You're not claiming credits for that?
RAVI: No. *To be clear*, we report it; we don't sell it. **The temptation to monetise avoided emissions through carbon credits** is one we've resisted, because the accounting is contested.
ANALYST: Governance. Your board has two independent directors out of seven.
GRACE: Three from next quarter. **The proportion of board seats held by directors with no financial interest in the company** goes to forty-three per cent.
ANALYST: *Let me rephrase that back to you:* you're moving from a founder-controlled board to a balanced one, slowly.
GRACE: *That's a fair way to put it.* Slowly, because **the risk of losing the founder's clinical vision in a rush to governance** is real.
ANALYST: Triple bottom line. People?
RAVI: **Staff turnover among clinicians in their first two years** — down from thirty to nineteen per cent. *What that means in practice is* patients see the same doctor twice.
ANALYST: *If I can summarise:* reach, avoided travel, an independent board, retained clinicians.
GRACE: That's exactly it. Four numbers, none of them revenue.

**The case.**
- *Speak:* Present three non-financial metrics for an organisation you know to a sceptical analyst. Each metric is one complex noun phrase; each time the analyst rephrases it, confirm or correct in one sentence.
- *Write:* Write the ESG page of a report: four metrics, each as a noun phrase of at least twelve words, each followed by a plain-language sentence beginning *In other words*.

**Homework.**
- *Do:* Write the ESG page: four metrics as noun phrases, each followed by *In other words*.
- *Self-check:*
  1. Head noun of: *a reduction in patient travel emissions attributable to remote consultation*
  2. Head noun of: *staff turnover among clinicians in their first two years*
  3. Head noun of: *the temptation to monetise avoided emissions through carbon credits*
  4. Head noun of: *the risk of losing the founder's clinical vision in a rush to governance*
  5. Head noun of: *the proportion of board seats held by directors with no financial interest in the company*
- *Key:* 1 reduction · 2 turnover · 3 temptation · 4 risk · 5 proportion

- `u03-opener` — a boardroom with a long table and seven chairs, three of them a different colour, a large window onto a map of scattered rural villages with thin lines running to one city; Grace in her cobalt coat and Ravi with his folder at one end, the analyst's silhouette with a tablet at the other; the three chairs cobalt

## Unit 4 · Supply Chain Shocks
*If you'd signed the two-year contract, your price would have been fixed.*

**Can do:** renegotiate a contract under pressure; use the third conditional to argue about what would have happened; push back firmly and politely, and leave the other side a way to say yes.

**Scenario.** The hardware supplier for Arden's home-testing kits offers sixty per cent of volume at thirty per cent more, blaming a chip shortage. Grace holds the supplier to the contract's notice period, Ravi refuses to fund the market, and Grace offers fifteen per cent from January with a two-year commitment — and mentions onshoring as the contingency she would rather not use.

| strand | |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | bottleneck, contingency, onshoring, shortfall, notice period, default, volume, price increase, commitment, assembler, sensor, chip shortage, lead time · hold (someone) to, source, fund, take (something) back |
| Grammar | third conditional for arguing about the past: *If you'd signed the two-year contract, your price would have been fixed. If we'd known about the bottleneck, we'd have chosen differently. If the chips had been your only issue, you'd have called us in March.* |
| Communication | renegotiating a contract under pressure |
| In the Room | pushing back firmly but politely — refusing without closing the door |

**Lead-in.** When did a supplier or a landlord last change the terms on you, and what did you do? Look at the picture. Who is under more pressure?

**Grammar in the dialogue.**
- **If you'd signed** the two-year contract last spring, **your price would have been** fixed.
- **If we'd known** about the bottleneck, **we'd have chosen** differently.
- **If the chips had been** your only issue, **you'd have called** us in March, when it started.
- **If we'd had** more notice ourselves, **we'd have given** you more.

**In the Room — phrase box.**
- *I appreciate you telling me straight, and I'm going to be equally straight.*
- *With respect, that's not our problem to fund.*
- *I'm not going to move on the quarter, and I'd rather not move on fifteen.*
- *I can offer …*
- *Of course. Take it back, and take this with it: …*
- *I would rather not use it.*

**Model dialogue.** *A renegotiation. The supplier's account director, Grace and Ravi.*

SUPPLIER: The chip shortage has hit the sensor line. We can deliver sixty per cent of your volume next quarter, at a thirty per cent increase.
GRACE: *I appreciate you telling me straight, and I'm going to be equally straight.* Sixty per cent at plus thirty is not a renegotiation, it's a notice of default.
SUPPLIER: **If you'd signed** the two-year contract last spring, **your price would have been** fixed. You chose flexibility.
GRACE: We did, and **if we'd known** about the bottleneck, **we'd have chosen** differently. But the contract we do have says ninety days' notice on price. You're giving me nine.
SUPPLIER: The market has moved.
RAVI: *With respect, that's not our problem to fund.* **If the chips had been** your only issue, **you'd have called** us in March, when it started.
SUPPLIER: **If we'd had** more notice ourselves, **we'd have given** you more.
GRACE: Then we agree on the principle. Three things. Full volume at the current price for this quarter — that's the contract. Fifteen per cent from January, not thirty. And if you can't deliver full volume, we source the shortfall from a Polish assembler, at your cost.
SUPPLIER: Fifteen is thin.
GRACE: *I'm not going to move on the quarter, and I'd rather not move on fifteen. I can offer* a two-year commitment from January, which is what you wanted last spring.
SUPPLIER: I'll need to take that back.
GRACE: *Of course. Take it back, and take this with it:* onshoring the sensor assembly is on our contingency list. *I would rather not use it.*
SUPPLIER: Understood. Friday.

**The case.**
- *Speak:* Your partner is a supplier cutting volume and raising prices. Hold them to the contract, argue the past with three third conditionals, refuse twice without closing the door, and end with an offer and a contingency.
- *Write:* Write the letter confirming the position after the meeting: what the contract says, what would have happened had they given notice, what you can offer, and the contingency, stated once.

**Homework.**
- *Do:* Write the letter confirming your position after the supplier meeting.
- *Self-check:*
  1. If you ___ (sign) the two-year contract, your price ___ (be) fixed.
  2. If we ___ (know) about the bottleneck, we ___ (choose) differently.
  3. If the chips ___ (be) your only issue, you ___ (call) us in March.
  4. If we ___ (have) more notice, we ___ (give) you more.
  5. ___ respect, that's not our problem to fund. (With / By)
- *Key:* 1 'd signed / would have been · 2 'd known / 'd have chosen · 3 had been / 'd have called · 4 'd had / 'd have given · 5 With

## Unit 5 · Empathetic Leadership
*Six people can't have left for six unrelated reasons.*

**Can do:** hold a difficult one-to-one about performance; use modals of deduction to say what must, may and can't have happened; listen actively and reflect back before proposing anything.

**Scenario.** A team lead has lost six of fifteen people since March and slipped three deadlines. Grace opens with what she has noticed, deduces rather than accuses, notices the silence, and finds that the lead has been doing two jobs since her deputy left. The first fix is a deputy, not a warning.

| strand | |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | psychological safety, attrition, quiet quitting, exit interview, deputy, workload, deadline, performance, structure, criticism, dread · raise (a problem), carry, slip, let (someone) go |
| Grammar | modals of deduction about the present and the past: *six people can't have left for six unrelated reasons; two of them may have gone for money; you must be carrying a lot; that can't have been easy; it could be a structure problem; you must have been dreading this meeting* |
| Communication | conducting a difficult one-to-one on performance |
| In the Room | active listening and reflecting back — noticing the pause and naming it |

**Lead-in.** Think of a time a manager assumed the worst about you. What would a better question have been? Look at the picture. Which chair is the manager's?

**Grammar in the dialogue.**
- Six people **can't have left** for six unrelated reasons.
- Two of them **may have gone** for money.
- **You must be** carrying a lot of this on your own. **That can't have been** easy.
- The attrition **may not be** a performance problem at all. **It could be** a structure problem that looks like one.

**In the Room — phrase box.**
- *I want to start with what I've noticed, and then hear how it looks from where you sit.*
- *So money was part of it.*
- *You went quiet there.*
- *It sounds like that landed.*
- *Is that close?*
- *Then here's what I'm hearing: …*

**Model dialogue.** *Grace's one-to-one with Jana, a team lead.*

GRACE: Thank you for making the time. *I want to start with what I've noticed, and then hear how it looks from where you sit.* Six people from your team of fifteen have left since March.
JANA: Yes.
GRACE: And three deadlines slipped. I'm not here to list them. I'm here because six people **can't have left** for six unrelated reasons.
JANA: It's not what you think. They were offered more money.
GRACE: *So money was part of it.* Two of them **may have gone** for money. The exit interviews say something else for the others: they didn't feel safe raising problems.
JANA: … That **might be** fair.
GRACE: *You went quiet there. It sounds like that landed.* **You must be** carrying a lot of this on your own.
JANA: I've been doing two jobs since my deputy left. I didn't want to say.
GRACE: **That can't have been** easy, and it **must have** made every problem feel like a criticism. *Is that close?*
JANA: Very close.
GRACE: *Then here's what I'm hearing:* the attrition **may not be** a performance problem at all. **It could be** a structure problem that looks like one.
JANA: And the deadlines?
GRACE: The deadlines are real, and we'll fix them together. But a lead **can't** run fifteen people alone, so the first fix is a deputy, this month.
JANA: I thought you were going to let me go.
GRACE: **You must have been** dreading this meeting. I'm sorry it took me three months to have it.

**The case.**
- *Speak:* Hold a one-to-one with a colleague whose work has slipped. Start with what you noticed, deduce with four modals, reflect back three times before you propose anything, and name the silence when it comes.
- *Write:* Write the note to file after the meeting: what was observed, what may and must have contributed, what was agreed — no blame, one action with a date.

**Homework.**
- *Do:* Write the note to file after a difficult one-to-one.
- *Self-check:*
  1. Six people ___ left for six unrelated reasons. (impossible)
  2. Two of them ___ gone for money. (possible)
  3. That ___ been easy. (impossible)
  4. You ___ been dreading this meeting. (certain)
  5. It ___ be a structure problem. (possible, present)
- *Key:* 1 can't have · 2 may / might have · 3 can't have · 4 must have · 5 could / may

## Unit 6 · Data Governance
*The lock on the door is fine; it's the copy of the key on the kitchen table.*

**Can do:** explain data compliance and risk to people who don't work in data; use defining and non-defining relative clauses to be precise about which data, which people and which rule; make a technical idea land with an analogy.

**Scenario.** Two things happened in a week: a researcher asked for anonymised records and someone scraped the public doctor profiles. Felix explains both with analogies — a street photo with the faces blurred, the phone book versus a photocopy of it — and Grace names the risk that actually keeps her awake: an exported spreadsheet on a personal drive. Beatriz has seen that one before.

## Unit 13 · Venture Capital
*Having doubled revenue two years running, we're raising forty million.*

INVESTOR: Seed?
RAVI: **Bootstrapped** for two years, then a two-million seed, then A, B, C. **Founded in** twenty nineteen with three doctors and a laptop.
INVESTOR: What kills you?
GRACE: Regulation changing faster than we can. **Being** a health company rather than a tech company, we carry that risk deliberately.
INVESTOR: And the outage in November?
GRACE: Cost us four hundred thousand and a week of trust. **Having published** the review, we lost fewer patients than the model predicted — three per cent, not eight.
INVESTOR: Valuation?
RAVI: *We'll come to that when you've told me what you think it's worth.*
INVESTOR: *I'll take that as a good sign. Next slide.*

## Unit 14 · The Talent War
*Bound to is a word I've heard before, in a company that didn't.*
