# Book 5 — The Long Game (C1/C2)

*Influencing, innovating and executive diplomacy.* Read `README.md` first:
the world, the cast, the strands, the grading and the artwork spec all live
there. This file is the fifteen units.

**The company.** Meridian Group, London and Zürich. Forty thousand people,
listed, a century old. An engineering group that built half of Europe's
substations and serviced its oil and gas fields, now moving — not fast
enough for some, too fast for others — into grid technology and renewables.
A board, a regulator, a union, and a shareholder with nine per cent and a
phone permanently at his ear. Larkline, the Copenhagen e-bike maker, is
bought in Unit 6; Arden Health was looked at and left alone.

**The year.** Mei-Lin Chao joins the executive committee as Chief Strategy
Officer in Unit 1, with a keynote to give and a chief executive who makes
her rehearse the silences. She defends the exit from fossil-fuel contracts
to a hostile board, runs a blameless post-mortem on a city that went dark,
pitches an incubator that threatens the core business, faces a walkout and
a regulator, and by Unit 15 is named Kwame's successor in his farewell
address. Kwame runs the group, Helena chairs the board, Ilse is the law,
Victor is the nine per cent.

**Grading.** C1 into C2. Dialogues 250–340 words, 12–20 lines, sentences as
long as the point needs. Everything is available — cleft sentences, the
subjunctive, inversion, ellipsis and substitution, register shifts inside a
single turn — and each unit still foregrounds its own point. See README §5.

**Artwork accent:** plum. Mei-Lin's plum umbrella is in every picture she is
in. Stem and slots: README §7.

## Unit 1 · Thought Leadership
*What this industry needs is not more generation. It's the wires.*

**Can do:** shape an industry keynote so that one sentence gets quoted; use cleft sentences to put the weight of a sentence exactly where you want it; command a room with a pause rather than a raised voice.

**Scenario.** Mei-Lin's first public act as Chief Strategy Officer is a keynote that sets an agenda for the whole grid industry. The night before, Kwame makes her rehearse it — stops her after the first sentence, cuts "I want to talk about", and teaches her to count three after the line that matters and look at one person when she says "will".

| strand | |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | paradigm, zeitgeist, discourse, disruptor, agenda, framing, generation, grid, substation, quotable, lean in · frame, own (the silence), name, change the subject |
| Grammar | cleft sentences that front the focus: *What this industry needs is not more generation. It's the wires that are the problem. The reason I'm nervous is … It's precisely because everyone is saying renewables that cables will land. What a disruptor does is change the subject. All we have to do is … It was Meridian that built half of Europe's substations. It is Meridian that will rebuild them. What they paid for is …* |
| Communication | delivering a visionary industry keynote — one that sets the discourse rather than joining it |
| In the Room | commanding a room and using pauses: the count of three, the one face, the silence you own |

**Lead-in.** Think of a speech you remember. What is the one sentence you remember from it? Look at the picture. Who is speaking, and who is listening for the pause?

**Grammar in the dialogue.**
- **What this industry needs** is not more generation. **It's the wires that** are the problem.
- **The reason I'm nervous** is that the zeitgeist says "renewables", and I'm about to say "cables".
- **It's precisely because** everyone is saying renewables **that** cables will land. **What a disruptor does** is change the subject.
- **It was Meridian that** built half of Europe's substations. **It is Meridian that** will rebuild them.

**In the Room — phrase box.**
- *You've given them nothing to hold. Start with the sentence you'd want quoted.*
- *Don't fill the silence after it. Count three.*
- *That pause is the paradigm shift; the sentence just names it.*
- *Slow the last one down. Look at one person when you say "will".*
- *Nobody paid to hear what you want.*
- *Once more from the top, and this time own the silence.*

**Model dialogue.** *The night before the keynote. Mei-Lin rehearses; Kwame listens.*

MEI-LIN: "Good morning. Today I want to talk about the grid, and about—"
KWAME: Stop. *You've given them nothing to hold. Start with the sentence you'd want quoted.*
MEI-LIN: **What this industry needs** is not more generation. **It's the wires that** are the problem.
KWAME: Better. Now *don't fill the silence after it. Count three.*
MEI-LIN: **What this industry needs** is not more generation. It's the wires. *(one — two — three)* We have spent forty years building power stations and forty minutes thinking about how the power gets home.
KWAME: There. You felt the room lean in? *That pause is the paradigm shift; the sentence just names it.*
MEI-LIN: **The reason I'm nervous** is that the zeitgeist says "renewables", and I'm about to say "cables".
KWAME: **It's precisely because** everyone is saying renewables **that** cables will land. **What a disruptor does** is change the subject. Say the next part.
MEI-LIN: **All we have to do** is treat the grid as the product, not the plumbing. **It was Meridian that** built half of Europe's substations. **It is Meridian that** will rebuild them.
KWAME: *Slow the last one down. And look at one person when you say "will".* Not the room — one person.
MEI-LIN: **It is Meridian** *(pause)* **that will rebuild them.**
KWAME: Good. Now the discourse point — you're framing the whole debate, so don't apologise for it. **What I'd cut** is "I want to talk about". *Nobody paid to hear what you want.*
MEI-LIN: **What they paid for** is to be told where the industry is going.
KWAME: And now you sound like someone who knows. *Once more from the top, and this time own the silence.*

**The case.**
- *Speak:* Write the first ninety seconds of a keynote that changes the subject in your industry. Rehearse it with a partner who stops you after every sentence without a cleft, and who counts the pauses aloud.
- *Write:* Write the keynote's opening page — three hundred words, eight cleft sentences — and mark the two pauses and the one line you will say to one person.

**Homework.**
- *Do:* Write the keynote's opening page with eight cleft sentences.
- *Self-check:*
  1. This industry needs wires, not generation. (What …)
  2. The wires are the problem. (It's …)
  3. I'm nervous because the zeitgeist says renewables. (The reason …)
  4. Meridian built half of Europe's substations. (It was …)
  5. They paid to be told where the industry is going. (What …)
- *Key:* 1 What this industry needs is wires, not generation. · 2 It's the wires that are the problem. · 3 The reason I'm nervous is that the zeitgeist says renewables. · 4 It was Meridian that built half of Europe's substations. · 5 What they paid for was to be told where the industry is going.

## Unit 2 · AI Governance
*Any model that scores a person must be explainable to that person.*

**Can do:** draft a company-wide AI ethics policy with the General Counsel; use advanced hedging and distancing to hold a position without over-claiming it; navigate a moral dilemma to a decision you can defend.

**Scenario.** Mei-Lin and Ilse test the draft policy against a real tool: an attrition model that flags engineers "at risk of leaving" — and that works better in London than in Zürich because it was trained on English. The dilemma: a prediction that produces its own outcome. The policy line that emerges: any model that scores a person must be explainable to that person, and the attrition flag is shown to the engineer, not the manager.

| strand | |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | algorithmic bias, black box, singularity, explainability, training data, attrition model, flag, self-fulfilling, defer to, regulator, licence · score, nurture, neglect, err on the side of |
| Grammar | hedging and distancing that keep a claim at the right strength: *one might argue; it would appear so; I'd be inclined to say; arguably; there's a risk that; I'm not sure I'd go so far as to call it; it could reasonably be described as; the model is said to be; it seems; it's at least possible that; which, I'd suggest; that may prove; one could argue* |
| Communication | drafting a company-wide AI ethics policy |
| In the Room | navigating moral dilemmas — naming the tension, choosing a side, saying which way you'd rather be wrong |

**Lead-in.** If a tool predicted you were about to resign, who should be told first — you, or your manager? Look at the picture. Which figure can see the flag?

**Grammar in the dialogue.**
- **One might argue** that's exactly such a decision. — **It would appear so**, though **I'd be inclined to say** the flag is an input, not a decision.
- **There's a risk that** the prediction produces the outcome.
- **I'm not sure I'd go so far as to call it** bias, but **it could reasonably be described** as unfair by design.
- The model **is said to be** more accurate for the London engineers than for Zürich. **It seems** the training data was mostly English-language.

**In the Room — phrase box.**
- *Which is the whole anxiety in miniature: …*
- *Here's my policy line: …*
- *I'd rather we erred on that side.*
- *One could argue … One could also argue …*
- *Put it that way in the draft.*
- *That's rather elegant.*

**Model dialogue.** *Mei-Lin and Ilse with the draft policy.*

ILSE: The draft says no decision about a person is to be made by a model alone. HR has an attrition tool that flags engineers "at risk of leaving". **One might argue** that's exactly such a decision.
MEI-LIN: **It would appear so**, though **I'd be inclined to say** the flag is an input, not a decision. Nobody is dismissed by a flag.
ILSE: **Arguably** not. But managers see the flag and stop investing in the flagged. **There's a risk that** the prediction produces the outcome.
MEI-LIN: A self-fulfilling black box. **I'm not sure I'd go so far as to call it** bias, but **it could reasonably be described** as unfair by design.
ILSE: Then there's the second dilemma. The model **is said to be** more accurate for the London engineers than for Zürich. **It seems** the training data was mostly English-language.
MEI-LIN: So **it's at least possible that** we'd be nurturing one office and neglecting another, on the advice of a tool that can't explain itself.
ILSE: *Which, I'd suggest, is the whole singularity anxiety in miniature:* not that the machine takes over, but that we defer to it.
MEI-LIN: **I'd tend to agree.** *Here's my policy line:* any model that scores a person must be explainable to that person, in plain language, on request.
ILSE: **That may prove** expensive. Half of what we license can't explain itself.
MEI-LIN: Then half of what we license doesn't get to score people. *I'd rather we erred on that side.* **One could argue** it's a competitive disadvantage.
ILSE: *One could. One could also argue* it's the first thing the regulator asks for in eighteen months, and we'll have it.
MEI-LIN: *Put it that way in the draft.* And the attrition tool? **I'd suggest** it stays — with the flag hidden from the manager and shown to the engineer.
ILSE: *That's rather elegant.* The person the prediction is about is the only one who sees it.

**The case.**
- *Speak:* Take a tool your organisation uses or could use that scores people — for hiring, credit, performance — and argue its dilemma with a partner: each position hedged to exactly the strength you can defend, and a policy line at the end.
- *Write:* Write the two-paragraph policy clause: what a model may and may not do with a person, hedged where the law is unsettled and unhedged where the company has decided.

**Homework.**
- *Do:* Write the two-paragraph policy clause on models that score people.
- *Self-check:*
  1. ___ might argue that's a decision. (One / Some)
  2. The model ___ said to be more accurate in London. (is / has)
  3. I'm not sure I'd go so ___ as to call it bias. (far / much)
  4. It could ___ be described as unfair. (reasonably / reasonable)
  5. That ___ prove expensive. (may / must)
- *Key:* 1 One · 2 is · 3 far · 4 reasonably · 5 may

- `u02-opener` — two office towers, London and Zürich, each drawn as a grid of lit windows, one grid clear and one slightly blurred, a single figure in one window holding a small flag; Mei-Lin with her plum umbrella and Ilse with her ring binder on a bridge between the towers; the flag plum

## Unit 3 · The Boardroom
*Shareholder primacy, properly understood, requires us to maximise value over the period our shareholders hold it.*

**Can do:** defend a controversial decision to a board; build complex dependent clauses that carry concession, condition and consequence in one breath; manage forceful interruptions without losing the argument or the room.

**Scenario.** Item four: the exit from the remaining oil-and-gas service contracts by twenty twenty-nine — a fifth of group revenue and a ninety-million penalty. Victor interrupts Kwame on the number, interrupts Mei-Lin on fiduciary duty, and threatens a proxy vote. Helena chairs. The three largest holders have already written in support.

| strand | |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | fiduciary duty, shareholder primacy, proxy vote, penalty, cost of capital, basis points, premium, the floor, call the question, holder, item · invoke, be obliged to, cap, entitle |
| Grammar | complex dependent clauses — concession, condition, time and relative clauses stacked before and inside the main clause: *Although these contracts represent a fifth of revenue, and although exiting carries a penalty that we estimate at ninety million, the board should note that every one expires within five years. Whereas the penalty is a one-off, the cost of staying is recurring: as long as we hold them, our cost of capital carries a premium which the banks have quantified. If fiduciary duty meant maximising this year's revenue, the board would be obliged to sell the grid division, which loses money today and which every analyst values above the rest of the group.* |
| Communication | defending a controversial decision to a board |
| In the Room | managing forceful interruptions — as the speaker, and as the chair |

**Lead-in.** When did you last have to defend a decision to people who could overrule you? What did they interrupt? Look at the picture. Who is chairing?

**Grammar in the dialogue.**
- **Although these contracts represent** a fifth of group revenue, **and although exiting them early carries** a penalty **that we estimate at** ninety million, **the board should note that** every one of them expires within five years.
- **Whereas the penalty is** a one-off, **the cost of staying is** recurring: **as long as we hold** those contracts, our cost of capital carries a fossil premium **which the banks have now quantified at** forty basis points.
- **If fiduciary duty meant** maximising this year's revenue, **the board would be obliged** to sell the grid division, **which loses** money today **and which every analyst in the room values** above the rest of the group combined.
- Shareholder primacy, **properly understood, requires** us to maximise the value of the company **over the period in which** our shareholders hold it — **which, for the pension funds Victor invokes, is** decades.

**In the Room — phrase box.**
- *Victor, you'll have the floor.*
- *May I answer that directly?*
- *Let her finish, and then you'll have five minutes, uninterrupted.*
- *You're entitled to. Before you do, you might consider that …*
- *They arrived this morning, and you'll have them before lunch.*
- *I'm going to call the question at three.*

**Model dialogue.** *The board. Helena chairs; Kwame and Mei-Lin present; Victor.*

HELENA: Item four. The proposal to exit the remaining oil-and-gas service contracts by twenty twenty-nine. Kwame.
KWAME: **Although these contracts represent** a fifth of group revenue, **and although exiting them early carries** a penalty **that we estimate at** ninety million, **the board should note that** every one of them expires within five years, and that none has been renewed at margin since—
VICTOR: Ninety million. Say that number again to the pension funds.
HELENA: *Victor, you'll have the floor.* Kwame.
KWAME: **Whereas the penalty is** a one-off, **the cost of staying is** recurring: **as long as we hold** those contracts, our cost of capital carries a fossil premium **which the banks have now quantified at** forty basis points on everything we borrow.
VICTOR: That's a theory. Fiduciary duty is not a theory. You are proposing to walk away from a billion in revenue because it embarrasses you at dinner parties.
MEI-LIN: *May I answer that directly?* **If fiduciary duty meant** maximising this year's revenue, **the board would be obliged** to sell the grid division, **which loses** money today **and which every analyst in the room values** above the rest of the group combined.
VICTOR: Don't lecture me on—
HELENA: *Let her finish, and then you'll have five minutes, uninterrupted.*
MEI-LIN: Thank you. Shareholder primacy, **properly understood, requires** us to maximise the value of the company **over the period in which** our shareholders hold it — **which, for the pension funds Victor invokes, is** decades, not a quarter.
VICTOR: Then let the shareholders decide. I'll put it to a proxy vote.
KWAME: *You're entitled to. Before you do, you might consider that* the three largest holders have already written to Helena supporting the exit, **provided the penalty is** capped at a hundred million.
VICTOR: … I wasn't shown those letters.
HELENA: *They arrived this morning, and you'll have them before lunch. I'm going to call the question at three.* Victor, your five minutes.

**The case.**
- *Speak:* In fours — chair, proposer, seconder, opponent — defend a decision that costs money now to save more later. The opponent interrupts three times; the chair manages each one differently; the proposer's answers are single sentences with at least three dependent clauses.
- *Write:* Write the board paper's one-page summary of the exit: four sentences, each carrying a concession, a condition and a consequence, and a final line that states what the board is asked to resolve.

**Homework.**
- *Do:* Write the board paper's one-page summary: four complex sentences and the resolution.
- *Self-check:*
  1. ___ these contracts represent a fifth of revenue, every one expires within five years. (Although / Despite)
  2. ___ the penalty is a one-off, the cost of staying is recurring. (Whereas / However)
  3. ___ we hold those contracts, our cost of capital carries a premium. (As long as / Unless)
  4. The board would be obliged to sell the grid division, ___ loses money today. (which / what)
  5. ___ the penalty is capped, the holders support the exit. (Provided / Despite)
- *Key:* 1 Although · 2 Whereas · 3 As long as · 4 which · 5 Provided

## Unit 4 · Geopolitics & Risk
*Had I found a scenario that didn't end in Zürich, I'd have led with it.*

**Can do:** run scenario planning for external shocks; use inverted conditionals — *had we known, were the tariff to double, should the port close* — for formal hypotheticals; read the subtext in what a government adviser actually said.

**Scenario.** Three scenarios in an hour: transformer tariffs double; a supplier country becomes an unfriendly jurisdiction; a single power dominates the supply chain. Ilse decodes the minister's adviser — "under continuous review" means decided, "prudent" means instruction — and Kwame spots the subtext in Mei-Lin's own presentation: three doors, one decision.

| strand | |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | protectionism, tariff, decoupling, hegemony, sanctions list, unfriendly jurisdiction, lead time, supply security, bridge (n.), capacity, hindsight · come to pass, scale, diversify, walk through |
| Grammar | conditionals with inversion in place of *if*: *Were that to happen, the Zürich plant becomes the only unaffected supplier. Should it happen in the next year, we can't scale in time. Had we invested in twenty twenty-three, we'd be the winner. Had we done so, the board would have called it protectionism-chasing. Were the sanctions list to expand, we'd have ninety days. Should that come to pass, access is the question. Were I the board, I'd ask … Had I found a scenario that didn't, I'd have led with it.* |
| Communication | conducting scenario planning for shocks |
| In the Room | interpreting subtext — what "under review", "prudent" and "we value your engagement" actually mean |

**Lead-in.** What is the one external event that would change your organisation's year, and does anyone have a plan for it? Look at the picture. Which of the three doors is open?

**Grammar in the dialogue.**
- **Were that to happen**, the Zürich plant becomes the only unaffected supplier in Europe. **Should it happen** in the next year, we can't scale it in time.
- **Had we invested** in Zürich capacity in twenty twenty-three, we'd be the winner of that scenario.
- **Were the sanctions list to expand**, we'd have ninety days.
- **Had I found** a scenario that didn't, I'd have led with it. — **Should the tariff land** before the plant is ready, what's the bridge?

**In the Room — phrase box.**
- *Which means it's been decided. "Under review" is what you say a month before.*
- *They're telling you to start moving now.*
- *Read the rest of it for us.*
- *Nobody in government says "prudent" by accident.*
- *There's your subtext: she's not presenting scenarios. She's presenting a decision with three doors.*
- *Guilty. But the doors are real.*

**Model dialogue.** *Scenario planning. Mei-Lin, Kwame and Ilse.*

MEI-LIN: Three scenarios, one hour. Scenario one: transformer tariffs double. **Were that to happen**, the Zürich plant becomes the only unaffected supplier in Europe. **Should it happen** in the next year, we can't scale it in time.
KWAME: **Had we invested** in Zürich capacity in twenty twenty-three, we'd be the winner of that scenario. We didn't.
ILSE: **Had we done so**, the board would have called it protectionism-chasing. Hindsight has a lower cost of capital.
MEI-LIN: Scenario two: decoupling. Our largest cable supplier is in a country that may become an "unfriendly jurisdiction". **Were the sanctions list to expand**, we'd have ninety days.
KWAME: What did the minister's office actually say?
MEI-LIN: The adviser said the relationship was "under continuous review".
ILSE: *Which means it's been decided. "Under review" is what you say a month before.* And "continuous" is a courtesy: *they're telling you to start moving now.*
KWAME: *Read the rest of it for us.*
ILSE: "We value Meridian's engagement" — that's a request for a meeting. "Diversification is prudent" — that's the instruction. *Nobody in government says "prudent" by accident.*
MEI-LIN: Scenario three: hegemony. A single power dominates the whole transformer supply chain. **Should that come to pass**, tariffs are irrelevant; access is the question.
KWAME: **Were I the board**, I'd ask why all three scenarios end in "build more in Zürich".
MEI-LIN: Because they do. **Had I found** a scenario that didn't, I'd have led with it.
ILSE: *There's your subtext, Kwame. She's not presenting scenarios. She's presenting a decision with three doors.*
MEI-LIN: *Guilty. But the doors are real.*
KWAME: Then let's walk through the one with the shortest lead time. **Should the tariff land** before the plant is ready, what's the bridge?

**The case.**
- *Speak:* Run three scenarios for an organisation you know — a tariff, a sanction, a dominant supplier — each opened with an inverted conditional. Your partner reads you a "diplomatic" message and you decode it line by line.
- *Write:* Write the one-page scenario memo: three scenarios, each with a *were/should/had* sentence and a bridge, and a final paragraph that admits which decision the scenarios are really about.

**Homework.**
- *Do:* Write the one-page scenario memo with three inverted conditionals.
- *Self-check:*
  1. If that were to happen, Zürich becomes the only supplier. (no *if*)
  2. If we had invested in 2023, we'd be the winner. (no *if*)
  3. If the sanctions list were to expand, we'd have ninety days. (no *if*)
  4. If the tariff should land early, what's the bridge? (no *if*)
  5. If I had found another scenario, I'd have led with it. (no *if*)
- *Key:* 1 Were that to happen, Zürich becomes the only supplier. · 2 Had we invested in twenty twenty-three, we'd be the winner. · 3 Were the sanctions list to expand, we'd have ninety days. · 4 Should the tariff land early, what's the bridge? · 5 Had I found another scenario, I'd have led with it.

## Unit 5 · Sustainable Capitalism
*We stop pretending it has a future and let someone monetise its past.*

**Can do:** pitch a large operational shift — net zero, divestment, circular materials — to a divisional chief who has to deliver it; use discourse markers to carry a complex argument through concession, contrast and consequence; counter scepticism with logic and, when logic isn't enough, with an experiment.

**Scenario.** Mei-Lin proposes net zero across Meridian's operations by twenty thirty-two, divestment of the coal-services unit and circular recovery of transformer steel. Gunnar, whose division makes the steel, doesn't buy the numbers. She grants, contrasts, and finally offers him a pilot he cannot lose. Kwame calls it what a Chief Strategy Officer is for.

| strand | |
|---|---|
| Vocabulary | decarbonisation, circularity, divestment, net zero, decommissioning, recovered steel, ore, carbon tax, buyer pool, basis points, pilot, clause · monetise, hold, shrink, withdraw |
| Grammar | discourse markers that steer a long argument: *Granted. Nevertheless, … By the same token, … That said, … In the first instance, … Conversely, … To put it bluntly, … Which is to say, … Admittedly. Be that as it may, … Moreover, … On the contrary — … In other words, … Provided …, … Rather, … Either … or …* |
| Communication | pitching a massive operational ESG shift to the people who must deliver it |
| In the Room | countering scepticism with logic — and turning the last objection into a test |

**Lead-in.** What does your organisation make or do that will not exist in ten years? Who is in charge of admitting it? Look at the picture. Which pile is being weighed?

**Grammar in the dialogue.**
- **Granted. Nevertheless**, the first decommissioning wave starts in twenty twenty-eight, and **by the same token** the company that can recover the steel is the company that gets the contract to remove the unit.
- **In the first instance**, ore is priced without its carbon. **Conversely**, recovered steel is priced without a carbon tax that is, **to put it bluntly**, coming whether we like it or not. **Which is to say**, the question isn't which is cheaper today.
- **Admittedly. Be that as it may**, it's shrinking eight per cent a year. **Moreover**, its presence in the group is what costs us the forty basis points.
- **On the contrary** — we sell it to someone who'll run it to the end. **In other words**, we stop pretending it has a future.

**In the Room — phrase box.**

## Unit 7 · Crisis Leadership
*Three things happened and none of them is a person.*

**Can do:** lead a crisis task force through a failure; use passive structures to report what happened without assigning blame, and know when to switch back to a name; project calm and extreme empathy to the person who thinks they are about to be walked out.

## Unit 15 · The Legacy
*A company is not what it makes. It is what it decides to stop making.*

**Can do:** deliver a farewell address that hands over rather than looks back; use complex syntax for rhetorical effect — the periodic sentence, anaphora, antithesis, the cleft in series; build a speech on the rule of three and know when to break it.

**Scenario.** Kwame's farewell to the group, in the Zürich plant hall. Three things he learned, because you remember three. Three things the company stopped making. An endowment of a hundred million for grid-engineering scholarships — long-termism with a name on it, and not his. And three sentences beginning "It was Mei-Lin who", ending with the board's decision that she should stand inside the building rather than outside it.
