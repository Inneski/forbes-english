Matching Headings

A Reading task whose list of answers follows no order — so the technique is different

C1 · Advanced Reading · both modules 18 points
Before you start

More headings than paragraphs, and no order to lean on

The shape of the task

A list of headings numbered i, ii, iii, and a passage with paragraphs lettered A to G. Each paragraph takes one heading. There are more headings than paragraphs, so some are never used — and they are written to be tempting.

Seven paragraphs, ten headings, three decoys. That is the usual arithmetic.

A type that jumps

Most question types follow the passage. Here the paragraphs run A, B, C, but the list of headings has no order: heading i can belong to paragraph F, and where a heading sits on the list tells you nothing about where to look.

It usually comes first on its passage, before the questions that do run in order.

Read the paragraph before the list

The trap is to read ten headings first and then hunt for them in the text — ten ideas in your head, all looking for a home. Read a paragraph, say in your own words what its main point is, and only then look at the list for the heading that says the same.

Your own summary first, the list second. Every time.

Before you start

Ask what the paragraph is doing, not just what it is about

Function over topic

Two paragraphs can share a topic and do different jobs with it: one introduces a problem, the next gives an example, a third weighs two views, a fourth proposes a fix. The heading states the main idea — often by naming the job. A heading that only names the topic fits half the passage.

Introducing, illustrating, comparing, warning, proposing. Five verbs cover most of what a paragraph does.

The topic sentence moves around

It is usually first, and the people who write these tests know you know that. A paragraph can open with an example and state its point at the end, or bury it in the middle after a concession. Read to the last sentence before you decide.

A heading that matches only the first sentence is one of the commonest traps.

A shared word proves nothing

The right heading may share the paragraph’s topic word, but it states the main idea. The decoys are built from details — a word or a fact from one sentence — so that a candidate scanning for words finds them.

Matching a word takes a second. Matching an idea takes longer. Take the time.

Before you start

Sure ones first, cross it out, two-fit goes last

Do the certain ones first

Some paragraphs have one obvious heading. Take them, whatever letter they carry. Every certain match shortens the list for the ones you are not sure of, and the hardest paragraph is often decided by what is left rather than by what it says.

Read in order if you like, but commit easy to hard.

Cross it out once used

No heading is used twice. Strike it off the list the moment you commit to it, and strike off the paragraph too. A list that still shows ten headings with two paragraphs to go is asking you to reconsider every decision you have already made.

Pencil, not memory. Under time you will forget which you have used.

When a heading fits two paragraphs

Leave it. Do the rest, and come back with the list shorter. Then ask of each paragraph: does this heading cover the whole of it, or just one sentence? One of the two has a better heading elsewhere, and elimination decides what reading could not.

Too general and too specific both fail. The right heading fits the paragraph and fits nothing else.

    🗣Discussion · in pairs

    In pairs, with any article to hand. One of you writes a heading for each paragraph, then adds two decoys that belong to no paragraph — one built from a detail, one too general. Shuffle the list, swap, and match. Then argue every match.

    • Whoever matches must say what each paragraph is doing — introducing, comparing, warning — before naming its heading.
    • For each decoy, say which paragraph it was written to tempt you towards, and what gives it away.
    • Find one heading in your partner’s set that fits two paragraphs, and rewrite it so that it fits only one.
    ✍️Writing · 150–200 words

    Take a passage of three paragraphs and write a heading for each. Under every heading, quote the one sentence that proves it is the main idea, and say in a line why the heading covers the whole paragraph rather than that sentence alone.
