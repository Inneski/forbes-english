Think It, Get It

Part 1 · the vocabulary of manifesting
The trend

Believe it and it will come

To manifest something is to bring it into your life by believing it will happen: picturing it, writing it down, saying it out loud. The idea is old, but The Secret (2006) made it a bestseller and TikTok made it a trend. In 2024 Cambridge Dictionary chose manifest as its Word of the Year.

Supporters call it focus. Critics call it wishful thinking with better marketing.

The trend

The vocabulary of manifesting

manifest (verb)

To bring something into your life by believing in it and picturing it. The older meaning is "to show clearly".

“She says she manifested her new flat.”

a vision board

A board of pictures and words showing the life you want, kept where you see it every day.

“Her vision board has a beach house, a dog and a promotion on it.”

an affirmation

A short positive sentence about yourself, repeated in the present tense as if it were already true.

“Every morning he repeats the affirmation ‘I am calm and confident.’”

The trend

The sceptic’s vocabulary

wishful thinking

Believing something because you want it to be true, not because the evidence says so.

“Expecting a pay rise without asking for one is wishful thinking.”

the law of attraction

The belief that positive or negative thoughts bring positive or negative events. No scientific evidence supports it.

“According to the law of attraction, worrying about debt brings more debt.”

a self-fulfilling prophecy

A prediction that comes true because believing it changes how people behave.

“Expecting to fail the interview became a self-fulfilling prophecy.”

Said to Work

Part 2 · reporting a claim with distance
The claims

Reporting a claim without believing it

Careful writers rarely say "manifesting works". They report the claim and keep their distance: It is claimed that picturing success brings it closer. Manifesting is said to work best if you feel grateful in advance.

The passive puts the claim into the sentence without putting your name on it. That is why the news, essays and reports use it so much.

The claims

Three patterns for a claim

It is said that + clause

The reporting verb in the passive, then the whole claim as a clause. Also: it is claimed / believed / thought / reported that.

“It is said that writing a goal down makes you more likely to reach it.”

X is said to + infinitive

The subject of the claim moves to the front. The reporting verb is passive; the infinitive carries the claim.

“Manifesting is said to work best if you feel grateful in advance.”

X is said to have + past participle

For a claim about the past, use the perfect infinitive: to have done, to have made, to have sold.

“The Secret is said to have sold over thirty million copies.”

The claims

Adverbs that keep their distance

supposedly

You doubt the claim, and you want the listener to know it.

“Supposedly, a glass of water can be ‘charged’ with your intentions.”

apparently

You are repeating what you heard or read, without judging it.

“Apparently, half her office has a vision board now.”

allegedly

A claim that has not been proved, usually about wrongdoing. Common in the news.

“The coach allegedly charged £5,000 for a weekend retreat that never happened.”

What the Evidence Says

Part 3 · hedging, and the psychology
The evidence

What the research says

The psychologist Gabriele Oettingen spent years testing positive thinking. People who only fantasised about success tended to put in less effort, and achieve less, than people who also pictured the obstacles in the way.

Her method, WOOP (Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan), keeps the dream and adds the plan. That part of manifesting seems to work.

The evidence

Saying what the evidence shows

There is little evidence that

A polite, precise way of saying a claim is probably false.

“There is little evidence that thoughts alone change events.”

It would seem that

Presents a conclusion carefully and leaves room to be wrong.

“It would seem that the planning, not the wishing, makes the difference.”

tends to

Describes a pattern, not a rule. Research rarely proves "always".

“Fantasising about success tends to reduce effort.”

The evidence

Why it seems to work

confirmation bias

Noticing the evidence that supports what you already believe, and forgetting the rest.

“She remembers the parking space she manifested, not the ten she didn’t.”

the placebo effect

Feeling better because you expect to, not because of the treatment itself.

“Part of the calm may be the placebo effect.”

correlation is not causation

Two things happening together does not mean one causes the other.

“Successful people keep journals, but correlation is not causation.”

The evidence

Fact, or kept at a distance?

Drag each sentence into the column it belongs in.

“Vision boards change your future.” “Vision boards are said to change your future.” “Gratitude attracts money.” “Supposedly, gratitude attracts money.” “The universe answers your thoughts.” “There is little evidence that thoughts change events.” “Affirmations always work.” “Affirmations tend to help some people relax.” “It would seem that the plan matters most.”

Somewhere in the Middle

Part 4 · giving a balanced view
Your view

Half right is not the same as right

Most people do not think manifesting is magic, or that it is nonsense. They think it is half right: writing a goal down helps; the universe does not. A balanced view concedes the good point first, then says where the idea falls down.

It is the shape of a strong discussion answer, and of a strong essay paragraph.

Your view

Giving a balanced view

There’s something to be said for

Concedes a point in favour before you criticise.

“There’s something to be said for writing your goals down.”

Where it falls down is

Introduces the weak point of an idea.

“Where it falls down is the idea that wanting is enough.”

I’d stop short of saying

Agrees in part, but refuses the strongest version of a claim.

“I’d stop short of saying it’s a scam, but it is oversold.”
