The Atlantic

One language, two shores. Most of English is the same on both sides of the ocean, but some harbours face each other with different names: an American takes the elevator, a Brit takes the lift. Read the chart across, harbour to harbour, then pick a shore and stay on it.

Reading the crossing

Same ocean, two shores

Neither shore is right or wrong. Both understand each other perfectly. The skill is choosing one shore and not switching in the middle of a text.

Different words, same thing

Everyday objects often have two names. Learn them in pairs, harbour to harbour.

We're going on holiday in August. / We're going on vacation in August.

Different spelling

Britain keeps -our, -re and a double l; America drops them: colour / color, centre / center, travelled / traveled.

What colour is the boat? / What color is the boat?

Gotten sails west only

Americans say has gotten for a change; Britain says has got. Both say have got for having.

She's got much taller this year. / She's gotten much taller this year.

The present perfect is busier in Britain

With just, already and yet, Britain uses the present perfect; America often uses the past simple.

Have you eaten yet? / Did you eat yet?

Small words differ

At the weekend in Britain, on the weekend in America. Britain also says different to; America also says different than.

What are you doing at the weekend? / What are you doing on the weekend?

A crew can be one thing or many

In Britain a group noun can take a plural verb (the crew are); in America it is almost always singular.

The crew are tired. / The crew is tired.

The Shallows and the False Cape

Shared words, and words that change at sea

In the Shallows, both shores are at home. At the False Cape a word lands on the other shore and means something else, which is where the jokes, and the embarrassments, come from.
