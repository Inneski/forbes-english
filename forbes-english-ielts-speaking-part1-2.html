Answering at Length

Speaking Parts 1 and 2 — how long an answer should be, how to extend one without waffling, and how to fill the two minutes of the long turn.

C1 Advanced Speaking Parts 1 & 2 17 slides
The test

Eleven to fourteen minutes, three parts

Part 1 — about twelve questions on three everyday topics. Part 2 — a card, one minute to prepare, then you talk. Part 3 — a discussion of the same topic, but abstract.

One test for Academic and General Training, marked identically. Four criteria, 25% each: Fluency & Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammar, Pronunciation. All four are about language.

Nothing here is marked on what you know. The examiner does not care whether your opinion is right, or whether the story is true. The topic is only an excuse to produce English.

Part 1

Answer, then keep going

Every Part 1 answer is two moves: answer the question, then give one reason, example or contrast. Two or three sentences. That is the whole shape.

One word leaves the examiner nothing to mark. A speech is no better — the examiner has about twelve questions to fit into five minutes, so you will be cut off, and the rest of what you planned is never heard.

Part 1 is the easy part and candidates still lose marks in it, almost always by answering as though it were a conversation with a stranger rather than a language test.

Part 2

One minute to prepare, two to talk

The card has a task and three or four bullets. They are a spine, not a checklist — you are not marked on ticking them off, and the last one is usually worth the most time.

Aim for the full two minutes. That is roughly 250 words at a natural pace — far more language than most candidates expect, which is why so many stop at forty seconds.

Use the minute for notes, not sentences. Anything you write as a full sentence you will read aloud, and read-aloud English sounds nothing like spoken English.

The second move

Four ways to keep going

Give a reason. …because I never learnt properly.

Give an example. …last week I made the same thing four times.

Compare with the past. …I used to cook a lot more before I moved.

Qualify it. …though it depends how much time I have.

The past comparison is the most useful of the four: it forces a second tense into an answer that would otherwise sit entirely in the present.

Question 5

Two words that buy you a clause

Both of these rescue an answer that came out too short.

Not really, I did enjoy it when I was younger.

That's a hard one — I suppose it on how busy the week has been.

Question 6

Opening and closing the long turn

Two sentences that make a two-minute talk sound organised rather than improvised.

The person I'm going to about is my old geography teacher.

I finish, I should say that I haven't seen her since I left school.

🗣Discussion · in pairs

One of you is the examiner and holds the clock. Swap after each task.

  • Ask six Part 1 questions about food, weekends and where your partner lives. Every answer must have a second move. Stop them the moment one does not.
  • Give them the card describe a person who taught you something. One minute of notes, then talk. Cut them off at exactly two minutes and say where they were.
  • Run it again, but this time interrupt twice with a question. The task is to answer and get back to the talk without losing the thread.
✍️Writing · 200–260 words

Write the one minute of notes for that card — words, not sentences — and then the full two-minute talk. Two hundred and fifty words is roughly two minutes. Write it out once so you know how much language that actually is.
