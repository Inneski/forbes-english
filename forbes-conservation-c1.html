Before the first question

Five of the six questions ask what the article implies

Not what it says

Recall questions have one findable answer. These do not.

You are being asked what follows from the text — which is why you can locate a sentence and still choose wrong.

The distractors are usually true

They are just not the article’s point.

Several name real problems that conservation projects elsewhere do face. Truth is not the test; relevance is.

The habit that helps

Ask what the sentence is doing, not only what it reports.

A detail can be there to prove a point, to concede one, or to set a scene. Which it is decides the answer.

Worked example

Two readings of the same sentence

The sentence

It could have vanished before science knew it existed.

About the Mashpi glass frog, documented after five years of survey work in the Chocó cloud forest.

The shallow reading

“A rare frog was found.”

True, and it answers nothing. It treats the clause as a fact about an animal.

The reading being tested

“Undocumented species are being lost, so the reserve is urgent.”

The clause is an argument for the reserve. That is what it is doing in the paragraph, and that is the answer.

The transferable one

Naming by comparison — “Europe’s Yellowstone”

What it imports

Scale, ambition, continental-scale rewilding, restored large mammals.

This is the load the phrase is meant to carry, and the article says so in the sentences around it.

What it does not

The car parks, the visitor numbers, the funding, the nationality.

A comparison never imports everything. If it did, it would be an identity claim rather than a comparison.

Deciding which

Read what the writer does with the phrase immediately afterwards.

The next sentences here are about reintroduced species and old-growth forest. So: ecology, not commerce. The context selects the load.

The vocabulary, one

Scarcity and loss

rare

Uncommon in itself. Rare high-altitude ecosystems.

Not scarce, which is about supply against demand, and not endangered, which is about trajectory.

face extinction

The collocation. All coral reefs could face extinction by 2050.

Of a species or a whole system. Face is doing work: it frames the outcome as still ahead and still avoidable.

millennia

Plural of millennium. Over two millennia.

A Latin plural that survived intact into formal English. Reach for it when the span itself is the point.

The vocabulary, two

Transformation and heritage

retraining

Retraining its loggers and poachers as researchers.

The prefix carries the argument: the skills were already there — the forest knowledge, the tracking — and were redirected, not replaced.

traditional

Traditional Polynesian knowledge, blended with marine science.

Neutral here, deliberately. Elsewhere it can imply outdated; set alongside modern science as an equal partner, it does not.

cultural

Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property — Japan’s formal designation for the ama.

An ordinary word inside a fixed official title. The capitals belong to the title, not to the word.

The five projects

What each one is actually protecting

Ecuador · Mashpi

A logging concession bought out and turned into cloud forest reserve.

24 new species recorded, the glass frog among them.

Romania · the Carpathians

Rewilding at scale — bison, lynx, old-growth forest.

The one the Yellowstone comparison is about.

Costa Rica · the turtles

Night patrols, nests relocated to guarded hatcheries.

Against poaching, with round-the-clock monitoring.

Polynesia & Japan

Coral Gardeners replant resilient coral; the ama dive on held breath.

One protects a habitat, the other a practice. The last activity asks you to tell those apart.

🗣Speaking · in pairs

One of you has funding for exactly one of the five projects. The other wants it spent somewhere else.

  • Argue for a project that protects a practice rather than a species. Notice that it is harder, and say why.
  • Use face extinction once, accurately — about something that can actually become extinct.
  • Make one claim your partner has to infer rather than one you state outright. Then ask them what they took from it.
  • Compare your project to a famous one. Then say which properties the comparison carries and which it does not.
