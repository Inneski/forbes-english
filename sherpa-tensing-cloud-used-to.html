Cloud briefing

When to use it

This is not a tense. It is one fixed form that exists only in the past and always means the same thing: this was true, and it is not true now. The gap between the block and NOW is the entire point of saying it.

A habit that has stopped

Something you did regularly and do not do any more.

"I used to smoke."

A state that has changed

Not an action at all — a way things were.

"There used to be a bakery on this corner."

Repeated over a stretch of years

The years are vague on purpose; do not date them.

"We used to walk to school every day."

The contrast is the message

You are not reporting the past. You are pointing at the change.

"I used to hate coffee."

Only ever the past

There is no present form. For a present habit, use the present simple.

"I drink coffee." not "I use to drink coffee."

Careful with the missing d

The d vanishes after did, exactly as in any past question.

"Did you use to live here?"
Building it

How it's built

Affirmative

subject + used to + infinitive

"She used to cycle to work."

Negative

subject + didn't use to + infinitive

"I didn't use to like it."

Questions

Did + subject + use to + infinitive?

"Did he use to smoke?"

Never changes

No person, no number, no -s. One form for everybody.

"They used to." · "He used to."

Where the d goes

The only spelling difficulty in the whole form. Did already carries the past, so the verb behind it drops back to its plain form — use, not used. It is the same rule as did you want, not did you wanted.

FormSpellingExample
Affirmativeused to"I used to run."
Negativedidn't use to"I didn't use to run."
Questiondid … use to"Did you use to run?"
Neverdid … used toThis is the commonest written error.

Used to or would?

Both can tell you about the old days, and they are not interchangeable. Would handles repeated actions; it cannot handle states. Used to handles both, which makes it the safer choice.

Meaningused towould
A repeated action"We used to walk there.""We would walk there." — fine
A state"There used to be a shop.""There would be a shop." — wrong
Living somewhere"I used to live in Rome.""I would live in Rome." — wrong
Opening a storyEither works.Common in writing: "Every summer we would…"

Three mistakes worth naming

Almost every error with this form is one of these three.

Not thisThisWhy
I use to play tennis.I used to play tennis. / I play tennis.There is no present use to. Decide which you mean.
I didn't used to like it.I didn't use to like it.Did carries the past, so the d comes off.
I used to live here for ten years.I lived here for ten years.A stated length of time makes it a fact, not a contrast. Past simple.
The fork in the path

Used to or past simple?

Both are past and both are true. The difference is what you are drawing attention to: the past simple reports, and used to points at the change.

Questionused toPast simple
What is it doing?Contrasting then with now.
"I used to live in Rome."		Reporting a fact.
"I lived in Rome for two years."
With a length of time?No. It loses the contrast.Yes. "for two years", "from 1998 to 2001".
A one-off event?Never. "I used to break my leg" is nonsense.Always. "I broke my leg in 2010."
A date?No.Yes. "I moved there in 1998."

The pair that shows it: "I lived in Rome for two years" tells you where I was. "I used to live in Rome" tells you where I am not. Same past, entirely different point.

Signal words

Words that go with it

  • back then · in those days
  • when I was younger
  • not any more · no longer
  • before · once
  • as a child
  • we always … in those days

Words that push you to past simple

  • for two years
  • from 1998 to 2001
  • in 1998
  • last summer
  • twice · three times
  • the day I arrived

A useful habit: say the sentence and then add … but not any more. If that sounds redundant rather than wrong, used to is the right form.
