Before the first question

This section is not testing whether you know these words

You already know them

Every word in the next seventeen items is one a C1 speaker has met before.

Not one of them is rare. That is deliberate, and it is the point of the exercise.

It tests which sense

Each item asks you to pick the right sense of a familiar word.

Usually the second sense — the formal, technical or idiomatic one, not the one you learned first.

Five words appear twice

report, critic, decay, reconcile, dwell.

Each is tested once in each sense, on consecutive slides. When you meet a word for the second time, ask what changed.

The transferable part

Three tells for picking the right sense

1 · The collocation

What sits next to the word.

A report circulates is a rumour. To report a sighting is an account. Same word, different company.

2 · The grammar

Noun or verb; transitive or not; which preposition follows.

Reconcile X with Y makes two things fit. Reconcile with Y repairs a relationship. Count the objects.

3 · The register

How formal the sentence around it is.

A formal sentence pulls a formal sense. In receipt of your letter is not the slip from a till.

Contrast one

report — three senses, and one of them is a sound

a report (noun)

An unverified account going round. A report is circulating that…

Also the formal written kind — the annual report. The article and the verb around it tell you which.

to report (verb)

To give an account of what you saw. Report any sighting within 24 hours.

Takes a direct object. Also report to someone — the person you answer to.

the report (of a rifle)

The sharp crack of a firearm. Two reports from the far ridge.

Rare in general English and routine in this one. On a reserve dealing with poaching and culls, it is the sense you will need in writing.

Contrast two and three

critic and decay — opinion, and word class

critic — the objector

Someone who says a thing is bad. A vocal critic on the council.

Unfavourable by definition. Comes with vocal, fierce, outspoken.

critic — the reviewer

Someone whose job is judging work. A critic for a magazine.

Carries no judgement at all. A critic in this sense can love everything they review.

decay — verb, then noun

Branches decay (the process). Visible decay (the condition).

The article is the tell. Adjectives such as visible or advanced can only modify the noun.

Contrast four

reconcile — the preposition tells you the sense

reconcile X with Y

Make two accounts fit. Reconcile the survey data with the landowner’s figures.

Two objects. Bookkeeping, evidence, competing versions of a number. Nothing to do with feelings.

reconcile with Y

Repair a relationship. Bergmann reconciled with the farmers’ union.

No direct object. People, or groups of them.

Why it generalises

Count the objects before you choose the sense.

The same test settles dwell on the next slide, and dozens of verbs beyond this lesson.

Contrast five

dwell — add a preposition, change the subject

dwell (somewhere)

To live in a place. Otters once dwelled along this river.

Formal and a little archaic. It survives mostly in dwelling, which is the word a planning document uses for a house.

dwell on (something)

To think or talk about it at length. Don’t dwell on the failed application.

Almost always something unhappy. To dwell on a success is to boast; the word expects a grievance.

Same tell as reconcile

One preposition moves the word from place to attention.

Two pairs, one rule. This is the tell worth carrying out of the lesson.

The pair that matters most here

prevalent or rampant — both mean widespread

prevalent

Common in a place or a time. Snaring is prevalent in this region.

Neutral. It reports a frequency and passes no judgement. Any disapproval in the sentence comes from other words.

rampant

Spreading unchecked, and unwelcome. Knotweed had grown rampant.

Carries two things at once: widespread, and bad, and nobody is stopping it. Never a compliment.

For a conservation agency

A native species is prevalent. An invasive species is rampant.

Choosing the wrong one in a site report either understates a problem or editorialises about a healthy population.

Register

The formal senses these same words carry

in receipt of

We are in receipt of your objection.

Formal acknowledgement that something arrived. Not the slip of paper — the same word doing near-legal work.

to launder

Funds laundered through a shell company.

From laundry. To wash money is to make illegal money look clean. Also airing dirty laundry — private disputes made public.

the decay of an institution

The slow decay of enforcement in the region.

The rot sense, applied to something abstract. This is the register a field officer writes reports in.

domineering

A domineering site manager.

Arrogant and controlling — the near-opposite of humble, which you will meet shortly.

🗣Speaking · in pairs

One field officer, one landowner. The survey figures do not agree.

  • Report a sighting formally, then report a rumour. Make the difference audible.
  • Say the snaring is prevalent. Then say the knotweed is rampant. Explain to your partner why you did not swap the words.
  • Reconcile your figures with theirs — then reconcile with them.
  • Disagree without becoming domineering.
