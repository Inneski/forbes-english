The Room

Read once for the story, then look out for the bold words — you'll be tested on them next.

Cafe Kowloon sits inside a railway arch in London Fields, east London, and it leads a kind of double life. During the day the room operates as Wonton Charlie's, a fast noodle counter for people passing through; once evening comes, the same space slows down and becomes the fuller Cafe Kowloon experience — two different rhythms of Hong Kong food culture sharing one address.

The interior design studio behind the project, Daytrip, was careful about what kind of homage this would be. Rather than reproducing a pastiche — a version that simply copies an existing style piece by piece — the team treated the old cha chaan teng tea-restaurant tradition as a starting point to build something new from, filtered through their own London perspective. The arch's raw, utilitarian shell stayed largely untouched: corrugated metal still runs across the walls and ceiling, doing the same practical job it always did.

Everything else gets layered on top of that industrial base. Brown mosaic tiles with a wet-look shine sit alongside timber panelling and a stretch of bright green fibreglass grating suspended overhead. Above the open kitchen, a stainless steel counter and a row of barstools with red seats occupy a slightly raised platform, putting the cooking on display rather than hiding it. In the main dining room, curved cherry-wood seating booths — finished in a warm, deliberately weathered leather for a rougher, more tactile feel under the hand — wrap around glossy red tabletops fitted with spinning lazy susans, each one catching light and throwing it back across the room.

Once the sun goes down, a neon sign takes over the green grating behind the bar, and softer, warmer lamps fill in underneath it so the mood stays ambient rather than harsh — even the bathrooms keep the theme going, lit entirely in red. The whole effect aims for something atmospheric, even a little cinematic: less a dining room built purely for function, more a set dressed for a specific scene. Nothing here is allowed to look too new or too perfect — finishes stay lo-fi on purpose, reflective in places but never fully polished.

Colour is really doing the heavy lifting in how the space reads. Keep the background grounded — muted browns, neutral tones, natural wood — and the jade green and cherry red have somewhere to land with real force: saturated, sudden, and concentrated at doorways, shelving edges, and tabletops, rather than spread evenly through the room. Calm everywhere first, then one sharp hit of colour exactly where your eye is meant to stop.

Based on reporting by Rheanna Hopkins, Dezeen, on Cafe Kowloon by Daytrip and 6 of 1. Paraphrased for classroom use.

Describe a Room You Know

No fixed answer here — just your own description.

Think of a room you know well — a cafe, a friend's kitchen, your own bedroom. Write a short description (100–150 words) using at least five of today's ten target words. Try to show, not just tell: instead of "the room felt atmospheric," describe what actually makes it feel that way.

Model paragraph My grandmother's kitchen was never glossy or designed to impress — it was purely utilitarian, built for cooking rather than showing off. But it always felt strangely atmospheric: the low, ambient light from one lamp in the corner, the tactile roughness of the wooden table worn smooth in patches, the grounded, quiet colours of old paint and copper pans. Nothing in it was saturated or loud. It was never trying to be cinematic. It just was what it was, and that was exactly why it felt like home.
