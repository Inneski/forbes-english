I
Film Criticism Terminology
Mise-en-scène · Diegesis · Verisimilitude · Denouement
C1 Theory

These are the terms film critics, academics, and industry professionals use precisely and confidently. A wrong word here is a serious loss of credibility. Choose the term that fits both the meaning and the register of the sentence.

01
Critical Terminology

The reviewer praised the director's control of ___ — the precise arrangement of every visual element within the frame: the actors' positioning, the set design, the lighting, and the costumes.

💡 Mise-en-scène (French: "placing on stage") is the overarching term for everything visible within the frame as a composed whole: setting, lighting, costume, actor placement, and movement. Cinematography refers specifically to camera work and lighting technique. Blocking refers only to actor and camera movement planning. Production design covers sets and visual environment alone.
02
Critical Terminology

The Wagner leitmotif plays whenever Nietzsche remembers his former friend. This music exists only on the film's soundtrack — the characters cannot hear it. It is ___ music.

💡 Non-diegetic sound exists outside the story world — only the audience hears it (a film score, voiceover narration). Diegetic sound exists within the story world and could theoretically be heard by the characters (a piano playing in a room, a gunshot). The distinction is fundamental in film sound theory.
03
Critical Terminology

Some critics felt the film sacrificed ___ for spectacle: the 19th-century Turin streets looked too clean, the costumes too pristine, and Nietzsche too healthy — none of it felt historically believable.

💡 Verisimilitude (from Latin verum similis — "like the truth") is the quality of seeming true or real within the conventions of the work — it is the preferred critical term over "realism," which implies a specific artistic movement. Authenticity is a broader cultural/ethical concept. Continuity refers specifically to visual consistency between shots, not believability. At C1, distinguishing verisimilitude from realism is essential.
II
Precise Collocations
The words that belong together in professional film English
C1 Collocation

At C1, vocabulary errors are rarely about meaning — they are about collocation: which exact verb, adjective, or preposition naturally accompanies a given noun in professional contexts. All four options may be close in meaning; only one is idiomatic.

"A film is never really good unless the camera is an eye in the head of a poet."

— Orson Welles
04
Verb Collocation

After three years of post-production disputes, the studio finally ___ the film for release last autumn.

💡 To greenlight a project (past: greenlit) is the standard industry collocation for giving official approval for a film to proceed — used specifically in the film and television business. "Authorised," "permitted," and "validated" are all technically possible but sound like a bureaucratic document, not a film industry professional speaking.
05
Noun + Preposition Collocation

The film went significantly over budget, and the producers had to seek additional funding ___ the eleventh hour to avoid shutting down the production.

💡 "At the eleventh hour" is a fixed idiomatic expression meaning at the last possible moment — the preposition is always "at." It comes from the Biblical parable of labourers hired at the last hour of the working day. At C1, mastery of fixed prepositional phrases in professional idioms is expected.
06
Adjective Collocation

The director's decision to cast an unknown actor in the lead role was considered ___ — no major studio had taken that risk on a period biographical film with a €40 million budget.

💡 Groundbreaking collocates naturally with creative or artistic decisions that open new territory — it carries a sense of innovation and courage. Unprecedented means "never done before" but is more formal and statistical. Unparalleled means "without equal" — a matter of quality, not novelty. Pioneering is close but more commonly used of people or movements, not single decisions.
III
Register & Connotation
Near-synonyms with very different weight and implication
C1 Nuance

All four options in each question are broadly similar in meaning. At C1, the task is not to understand the words but to feel their weight — their register (formal, neutral, pejorative, elevated), their connotation (positive, neutral, negative), and their appropriateness to the professional film context.

07
Connotation — Describing a Film Negatively

A Frankfurter Allgemeine critic writes of the film: "Despite its ambitions, Thus Spake Nietzsche is ultimately a ___ work — it imitates the surface of its influences without understanding them."

💡 Ersatz (borrowed from German into English) means a poor or inferior substitute — it carries a connotation of something that pretends to be the real thing. In intellectual criticism, "ersatz" implies fraudulence, not just lack of originality. It is the most precisely contemptuous of the four. Derivative is common but softer — merely derived from something else. Imitative is neutral and descriptive. Unoriginal is the most mundane register.
08
Register — Formal Written Context

In the official press release, the studio announced that the director would ___ from the project due to creative differences, and that a replacement would be named shortly.

💡 Withdraw is the correct formal register for a press release — neutral, professional, and non-judgmental. Walk away is informal and implies a degree of choice or principle. Bail out is informal/colloquial with a connotation of abandonment or cowardice. Quit is informal and slightly blunt. Register matching is a core C1 skill: the right word in the wrong register is still the wrong word.
09
Connotation — Critical Praise

The performance by the lead actor was widely described as ___ — a word that suggests the performance will be studied, referenced, and judged against for generations to come.

💡 Definitive means the best, most authoritative version — the standard by which all others will be measured. "The definitive Hamlet." It carries a sense of finality and critical consensus. Memorable simply means unforgettable. Outstanding means very good but not uniquely authoritative. Powerful describes emotional impact, not canonical status.
IV
Phrasal Verbs & Idioms in Context
Industry-specific idiomatic English used by professionals on and off the set
C1 Idiom

The following sentences are taken from pitch meetings, production emails, and festival interviews. Identify the correct phrasal verb or idiom — wrong choices will either sound unnatural or change the meaning entirely.

10
Phrasal Verb — Production Context

The production designer warned the director that if the budget was cut any further, they would have to ___ several key set pieces that were central to the visual identity of the film.

💡 Do away with means to abolish or eliminate entirely — the strongest and most definitive of the options. It conveys that something is being removed permanently and decisively, which fits the severity of losing "key set pieces." Cut down on means to reduce (not eliminate). Put off means to postpone. Get rid of is correct in meaning but too informal for the professional register of this sentence.
11
Idiom — Festival Pitch Context

In the pitch meeting, the executive producer made it clear that the studio would not ___ any further delays — the film had to be delivered to the festival by March 1st, without exception.

💡 Countenance (formal verb: to accept or permit something to happen) is the most precise fit for a formal professional ultimatum — it implies a refusal to permit or sanction. Put up with and stand for mean to tolerate — both slightly less formal and more personal in tone. Go along with means to agree or cooperate, which changes the meaning — it implies acceptance of an ongoing arrangement, not tolerance of a delay.
12
Idiom — Critical Interview

When asked about the film's controversial ending, the director said she had deliberately ___ easy resolutions, preferring to leave the audience with the discomfort Nietzsche himself lived with.

💡 Eschewed is the most precisely elevated choice — it means deliberately abstained from, often for principled reasons. It is the word a director speaking at a festival Q&A or to a quality broadsheet journalist would reach for. Avoided is neutral and too general. Shunned implies emotional rejection or social avoidance. Refrained from is correct but imposes self-restraint rather than artistic principle.
V
Word Formation in Context
Affixation, nominalisation, and lexical derivation in film English
C1 Morphology

C1 learners are expected to flexibly derive nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs from a root form. Each question gives you a root word in brackets. Choose the correct derived form for the gap. Incorrect affixes or forms will make the sentence grammatically or stylistically wrong.

portray(verb)
to depict or represent a character, place, or situation in a work of art.
13
Word Formation — Nominalisation

Critics debated whether the film's ___ of Nietzsche as a tragic romantic figure was historically responsible or dangerously misleading. (ROOT: portray)

💡 Portrayal is the correct nominalisation of "portray" — meaning the act or manner of depicting someone. Portraying is a gerund (verb form as noun) — grammatically possible but stylistically weaker in formal criticism. Portrait is a related noun but refers to a likeness (painting or photograph), not an act of representation in a film. Portrayal's is incorrectly possessivised here.
14
Word Formation — Adjective Derivation

The film's score was praised for its ___ use of Wagner's motifs — stripped of their nationalist associations and reworked into something quietly devastating. (ROOT: evoke)

💡 Evocative is the correct adjectival form — meaning tending to bring strong feelings, memories, or associations to mind. The suffix -ative forms adjectives from verbs: evoke → evocative, communicate → communicative, demonstrate → demonstrative. "Evocating" does not exist. "Evocational" is not a standard English form. "Evoked" is a past participle — grammatically possible as a modifier but changes the meaning to "having been evoked" rather than "capable of evoking."
15
Word Formation — Adverb in Critical Writing

The film's final sequence — Nietzsche weeping over a horse in the piazza — was handled ___, resisting both sentimentality and exploitation in equal measure. (ROOT: restraint)

💡 All four options are grammatically possible, but at C1 the question is also one of style and register. "With consummate restraint" is the most elevated and precisely appropriate for formal film criticism — consummate means showing great skill or completeness, and collocates powerfully with abstract nouns like restraint, artistry, and craft. "Restrainingly" sounds unnatural. "With restraint" is correct but plain. "In a restrained manner" is correct but verbose and pedestrian for critical prose.
