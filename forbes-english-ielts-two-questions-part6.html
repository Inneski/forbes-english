Two Questions, One Essay

The last of the five types — and a way to tell any of them apart in the thirty seconds before you start writing

C1 · IELTS Writing Task 2 Direct question essays All five types compared
The fifth type

Two questions, and both are marked

A direct question prompt asks two separate things. Each gets its own body paragraph, and the two should come out roughly the same size.

The failure is almost always the same: the first question is answered at length, and the second gets two sentences at the end.

The second question usually asks for a judgement — positive or negative, is it a problem, should it continue. That is where the marks for position live.

The essay · Direct question

Count the questions first

In many countries people are choosing to live alone in greater numbers than ever before. Why is this happening? Do you think it is a positive or a negative development?

Question one is causal. It wants explanation, not opinion — and two causes explained beat five listed.

Question two is a judgement. "Both" is not an answer to it. Choose, and say what you weighed to get there.

Band 9 · paragraph 1

Answer both, in miniature

Single-person households have become the fastest-growing type in much of the developed world. The change has been driven chiefly by rising prosperity and by later marriage, and while it carries real risks for the isolated, I regard it on balance as a positive development.

Both answers are already here. The causes in six words, the judgement in one clause. The body paragraphs will now prove what has been claimed.

"On balance" signals a judgement that has costs attached, which is what stops it reading as a slogan.

Question 1

Why answer in the introduction?

This introduction answers both questions before the essay has argued anything. What does that gain?

Band 9 · paragraph 2

Question one: two causes, worked

The most straightforward cause is that living alone has become affordable for people who would once have had no choice but to share. A generation of women in secure employment, in particular, can now support a household on a single income, and many are choosing to. Marriage has also moved later: the years between leaving a parental home and forming a partnership have stretched from two or three to a decade or more, and somebody has to live somewhere during them.

Two causes, each traced to a mechanism. Not "people are more independent" — which is a description of the trend wearing the costume of an explanation — but money, and a specific stretch of years that did not use to exist.

Band 9 · paragraph 3

Question two: the judgement, costed

The obvious danger is loneliness, and it would be dishonest to wave it away: living alone in later life, without a network, does measurable harm. But most people who live alone are not socially alone — they have moved their social life outside the front door rather than lost it. Set against the risk is something rarely counted: the number of unhappy or controlling households that no longer have to hold together for want of anywhere else to go.

The strongest objection is met first, at full strength. Then it is narrowed — the harm is real but concentrated in old age — and answered with a benefit the other side never counts. That is a judgement, not a preference.

Question 2

Opening with the objection

The writer thinks the trend is positive, yet begins the judgement paragraph with its biggest danger. Why?

Band 9 · paragraph 4

Both answers, one last time

The rise in solo living is mostly a consequence of people being able to afford what they already preferred, which is rarely a bad sign. Its dangers are real but concentrated in old age, and that is an argument for building community around the elderly, not for regretting the freedom.

Sentence one settles the cause; sentence two settles the judgement. Both questions are answered again, in order, in fifty words.

The last clause converts the objection into a recommendation — which is a stronger finish than repeating that the writer was right.

Band 6 · same question

Four causes, none explained

There are many reasons for this. Firstly, people are more independent now than in the past. They want their own space and to do what they want without asking anybody. Secondly, many people are focused on their career so they do not have time for a family. Also, divorce is more common nowadays. Another reason is that people can afford it because salaries are higher.

"People are more independent" is the trend, not its cause. It describes living alone in different words and calls that an explanation. Four reasons are named and not one is traced to anything — the paragraph is a list wearing the shape of an argument.

Band 6 · same question

And the second question gets this

In my opinion, this trend has positive and negative sides. It is positive because people are free and they can be happy. It is negative because they can feel lonely and this is bad for mental health.

In conclusion, there are many reasons why people live alone nowadays, such as independence, career and divorce. I think it is both positive and negative and it depends on the person.

Three sentences against the first question's six — and no judgement in any of them. "It depends on the person" answers nothing, and the conclusion spends half its length re-listing the causes instead.

Question 3

Two faults, one worse

The band 6 answer both under-develops its causes and refuses the judgement. Which costs more, and why?

The whole set · 1 of 2

Five types, five instructions

Opinion. "To what extent do you agree or disagree?" One position, stated early, held to the end.

Discussion. "Discuss both views and give your own opinion." Both sides fairly, then your own.

Advantages and disadvantages. "Do the advantages outweigh…?" Both sides weighed, and a verdict on which is heavier. Without outweigh in the prompt, both sides are enough.

The whole set · 2 of 2

And the two that come in halves

Problem and measure. "What problems… what measures?" Two halves, and every measure aimed at a problem you raised.

Direct question. Two questions, one paragraph each, roughly equal — and the second almost always wants a judgement.

One habit covers all five: before writing, count the things the prompt obliges you to produce. Then count them again at the end.

Question 4

Count what it obliges you to produce

“Some people think zoos should be closed. Others believe they protect endangered species. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.” How many separate things must this answer contain?

🗣Discussion · in pairs

Answer both halves aloud, and keep them the same length.

  • Why do fewer young people learn to drive than twenty years ago — and is that a good thing? Two answers, one minute each.
  • Your partner gives you a cause that is really just the trend reworded. Catch it, and push them for the mechanism underneath.
  • Take a verdict you hold strongly. State its strongest objection first, at full strength, and then answer it.
