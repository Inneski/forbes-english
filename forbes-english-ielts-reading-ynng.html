Yes, No, Not Given

The same three-way choice as True, False, Not Given — asked about what the writer thinks, not what the passage says

C1 · Advanced Reading · both modules 18 points
Before you start

Same three-way choice, a different question

Views, not facts

True / False / Not Given asks what the passage says. Yes / No / Not Given asks what the writer thinks. The instructions give it away: Do the following statements agree with the views of the writer?

Sometimes the wording is claims rather than views. The method is the same.

Where you meet it

In passages that argue: opinion pieces, reviews, essays with a point to make. You will meet it most often in the last section, the one most likely to argue.

Write the word the instructions ask for. YES on a True / False / Not Given question is marked wrong, even when you meant TRUE.

Same check as True / False / Not Given

Can I point at the sentence? Only now it has to be a sentence in which the writer speaks. If the only line you can find is someone else’s opinion, you have not found the answer yet.

YES and NO need the writer’s voice. NOT GIVEN is the writer’s silence.

Before you start

Whose voice is it?

Views the writer reports

Critics argue, it is often claimed, according to, many people believe. The view is in the passage, but the writer has not signed it. On its own, it can only give you NOT GIVEN.

These sentences state the claim in full, so word-matching finds them first. That is exactly what makes them traps.

The writer’s own voice

I think, in my view, the truth is — and far more often, no marker at all. In a passage that argues, a plain statement with nobody else’s name on it is the writer’s.

Most markers point at other people’s views; most of the writer’s own views carry none.

Reported, then judged

Supporters say it works. They are right. The second sentence makes the view the writer’s own. They are wrong would make the opposite the writer’s view.

One word can do it: rightly, wrongly, mistakenly.

Before you start

Admittedly … but

The concession

Admittedly, of course, it is true that, this is not to say (which concedes the opposite of what follows it). The writer grants the other side a point — and it is still the writer speaking. A point conceded is a point the writer accepts.

Contradict a concession and the answer is NO, even when the statement sounds like the writer’s side.

The turn

But, yet, even so, still. What follows is the writer’s main claim. It outweighs the concession; it does not cancel it.

Admittedly, the trial was small. But its results were clear enough to act on. The writer holds both.

What neither half says

The turn invites you to finish the argument for the writer. Do not. A statement that goes further than either half — about what happened next, or what it all adds up to — is NOT GIVEN.

Cheaper to run is not cheaper overall. Only one of them is on the page.

Before you start

How strongly does the writer mean it?

Degree

Largely, mostly, partly, to some extent. The writer is claiming part of something. A statement claiming all of it — completely, in every way, everything — is NO.

The same rule as most against all on a True / False / Not Given paper.

Hedges

May, might, perhaps, it seems. A hedge says the writer thinks something possible, not that it is true. A statement making the claim outright is usually NOT GIVEN: the writer has neither agreed nor disagreed.

Not NO. The writer has not denied it, only stopped short of promising it.

Evaluation words

Surprisingly, sadly, rightly, was right to. One word gives you the writer’s attitude, and statements test it: surprisingly agrees with unexpected.

The word judges one thing. Approving of a decision says nothing about the reason for it.

    🗣Discussion · in pairs

    In pairs, with an opinion piece to hand — a newspaper column or a review will do. Each of you writes four statements about it: one YES, one NO and two NOT GIVEN, one of them built on a view the writer only reports. Swap and answer. Defend each YES or NO with the sentence in which the writer speaks; for a NOT GIVEN, show that there is none.

    • Before a YES or a NO counts, say whose voice the deciding sentence is in.
    • Find the writer’s concession, if there is one, and write one statement it makes YES and one it makes NO.
    • Take a hedged sentence and say what the writer would have had to write for your statement to be YES.
    ✍️Writing · 150–200 words

    Write a short opinion paragraph, about a hundred words, on a question you care about. Give it a reported view, a concession and a turn. Then write three statements about it — one YES, one NO, one NOT GIVEN — and the key, quoting the words that decide each one.
