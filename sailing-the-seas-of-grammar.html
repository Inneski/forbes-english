Gerunds & infinitives

Sailing the Seas of Grammar

Two shores face each other across one narrow strait. Every harbour on the western shore takes the -ing form; every harbour on the eastern shore takes to + infinitive. No rule of logic decides which verb belongs where — it is geography, and you learn it the way sailors learn a coast. What you can learn as rules are the three things in the water between them: the current, the island, and the cape flying a false flag.

Examples in
The western shore

When you take the -ing form

A gerund is a verb wearing a noun's coat. Once you see it as a noun, four of these six stop being rules and start being obvious — nouns go after prepositions, nouns go at the front of sentences, nouns are what you avoid and enjoy and finish.

After the verbs of Gerundia

A closed list you learn rather than work out: avoid, enjoy, finish, mind, suggest, admit, deny, keep, practise, risk, consider, miss, imagine, postpone, give up.

She avoided answering the question.

After every preposition, without exception

This is the current on the chart. If a preposition comes first, the verb after it can only be -ing.

He left without saying goodbye.

As the subject of a sentence

English will not start a sentence with a bare verb, so the verb puts on its noun coat.

Sailing in fog is dangerous.

After go, for activities

Sports and pastimes you go out and do. Go sailing, go fishing, go shopping, go walking.

We went swimming before breakfast.

After no use, no point, worth

A small family of fixed expressions that all behave the same way.

There's no point arguing with the tide.

After a possessive, in careful English

my, your, his, the captain's. Spoken English usually drops to the object form, and nobody minds.

I don't mind your borrowing the chart.

The harbours of Gerundia

Grouped so they are easier to hold. Nothing joins a group for a reason — the groups are a memory aid, not a rule.

The eastern shore

When you take to + infinitive

The infinitive leans forward. Look at the verbs that take it — want, hope, plan, decide, promise, agree, refuse, expect — and almost every one of them is about something that has not happened yet. That is not a rule you can rely on, but it is a useful instinct while the list is still settling.

After the verbs of Infinitivia

The other closed list: want, decide, hope, refuse, promise, agree, manage, offer, learn, seem, afford, pretend, expect, plan, fail, choose.

They decided to turn back.

After a verb and its object

Somebody does something to somebody else: tell, ask, want, expect, advise, allow, remind, warn, teach.

He told me to wait on deck.

After an adjective

easy, hard, glad, ready, lucky, difficult, important — the infinitive says what the adjective is about.

It was hard to read the chart.

To say why you did something

The purpose infinitive. It answers what for? and it is the commonest infinitive of the lot.

I climbed the mast to see the shore.

After a question word

what, where, when, how, whether + to + infinitive, when the question is unresolved.

Nobody knew what to do.

After too and enough

too + adjective + to, or adjective + enough + to.

The sea was too rough to sail.

Verb, then object, then to

These verbs need somebody in the middle. The pattern is fixed: you cannot say he told to wait — English wants to know who was told.

VerbPatternExample
telltell + somebody + toThe captain told us to go below.
askask + somebody + toShe asked me to hold the rope.
want / expectwant + somebody + toThey wanted him to stay aboard.
advise / warnadvise + somebody + toHe warned them not to swim there.
remind / teachremind + somebody + toRemind me to check the fuel.

The bare infinitive: no to at all

A short list where the to disappears. Getting these wrong is the most audible error in this whole area, because she let me to go stops a listener dead.

AfterFormExample

        The False Cape

        When to is not an infinitive

        Here is the single most useful thing on this page. In look forward to, be used to and their relatives, the word to is a preposition, not the front half of an infinitive — so the current applies and the verb after it can only be -ing. The cape looks like it belongs to the eastern shore. It does not.

        The test that settles it

        Put it after the to. If the sentence still works, the to is a preposition, and whatever follows takes -ing.

        PhraseDoes … to it work?So you say
        look forward toI look forward to it. ✓looking forward to seeing you
        be used toI'm used to it. ✓used to sailing at night
        object toThey objected to it. ✓objected to being woken
        get round toI'll get round to it. ✓get round to fixing it
        when it comes toWhen it comes to it… ✓when it comes to navigating
        wantI want to it. ✗want to leave

        The other traps on this coast

        All of them are prepositions wearing the same coat.

        PhraseWrongRight
        be used toI'm used to sail at night.I'm used to sailing at night.
        get used toYou'll get used to work nights.You'll get used to working nights.
        look forward toI look forward to hear from you.I look forward to hearing from you.
        in addition toIn addition to carry the mail…In addition to carrying the mail…
        confess toHe confessed to take the chart.He confessed to taking the chart.

        One more thing worth separating. Used to and be used to are different words that happen to look alike. I used to sail is a past habit and takes the infinitive. I'm used to sailing is a state of familiarity and takes -ing. The difference is the verb be, and it is the whole difference.

        I used to sail here.  ·  I'm used to sailing here.

        The strait

        Twofold Isle: the same verb, two meanings

        These verbs take both forms, and the form changes what the sentence means. This is the only part of gerunds and infinitives where getting it wrong changes the facts rather than just the grammar — which is why it is the part worth learning properly. The pattern underneath is consistent: the -ing form looks back at something real that happened, and the to form looks forward to something intended.

        VerbWith -ing — looking backWith to — looking forward

        The Shallows

        Where the two channels run together

        South of the island the water shoals, the two channels merge, and for a short stretch it stops mattering which one you were in. After the verbs below both forms are correct and mean the same thing. This is not a third rule to learn — it is the absence of one, and it is the only water on the chart where a guess cannot sink you.

        That is worth saying plainly, because everything else on this page is a list you have to memorise. Here you do not. If you cannot remember whether begin is a western verb or an eastern one, the answer is that it does not matter.

        The verbs in the Shallows

        The shoal is small, and this is all of it. Nothing else on the chart is safe to guess at.

        VerbBoth of these are rightAny difference?
        begin · startIt began raining. / It began to rain.None whatsoever.
        continueShe continued walking. / to walk.The infinitive reads slightly more formal in writing.
        like · love · hateI like sailing. / I like to sail.A shade: -ing leans toward enjoying it, to toward choosing it.
        preferI prefer walking. / to walk.None.
        can't bearI can't bear waiting. / to wait.None.
        botherDon't bother waiting. / to wait.None.
        intend · proposeThey intend selling. / to sell.Both work; the infinitive is far commoner.
        attemptHe attempted swimming. / to swim.Both work; the infinitive is far commoner.

        Four things worth knowing before you relax

        None of these makes the Shallows dangerous. They are the buoys, not the rocks.

        The pointNot thisThis
        Never stack two -ing formsIt's starting raining.It's starting to rain.
        After would, only the infinitiveI'd like sailing.I'd like to sail.
        Before a state, prefer the infinitiveI began understanding.I began to understand.
        Comparing two things: -ing, then toI prefer to walk than drive.I prefer walking to driving.

        The would one is the trap. I like sailing and I'd like to sail sit one word apart and take different forms, because would like is not really the verb like any more — it is a polite way of saying want, and want lives on the eastern shore.

        I like sailing.  ·  I'd like to sail.

        And two verbs that feel as though they belong here but do not. Enjoy never takes the infinitive — enjoy to swim is simply wrong — and want never takes -ing. Being comfortable with a verb is not evidence that it lives in the Shallows. If it is not in the table above, it has a shore.

        I enjoy swimming.  ·  I want to swim.

        Tide tables

        Signal words

        Currents running west — take -ing

        • after any preposition
        • after to when it is a preposition
        • as the subject
        • after go + activity
        • after no use · no point · worth
        • after spend / waste + time

        Ropes pulling east — take to

        • after an adjective
        • to say why (purpose)
        • after a question word
        • after too and enough
        • after verb + object
        • after would like / love / hate

        One line to keep: a preposition is a current and it only runs one way. If you can find a preposition in front of the gap, stop looking for a rule — it is -ing.
