Advanced Grammar in Context

Reported speech, unreal conditionals, cleft sentences and complex passives — through Clarkson, Hammond, May and the Stig

B2 · Grammar Four structures 45–55 minutes 37 slides
Orientation

Four structures, one programme

Reported speech

What somebody said, folded into a sentence of your own: He said that he was leaving. The tenses usually step back one.

Unreal conditionals

Two of them here: the third (if + past perfect → would have) and the mixed, where a past condition has a result that is true now.

Cleft sentences

A sentence deliberately split in two so that one part takes the spotlight: It was the Stig who…, What I like is…

Complex passives

Reporting what people say without naming any of them: It is thought that…, The show is said to have…

Every rule tested later is stated on a slide first. Fifteen slides of language, twenty questions, then a speaking and writing task.

Orientation

The words this lesson uses

reporting verb

the verb carrying the report: say, tell, announce, claim, admit, deny

backshift

the step back in tense a report takes after a past reporting verb

perfect infinitive

to have + past participle — for an event earlier than the report

cleft

a sentence split in two so that one part is pushed into the spotlight

focus

the part the cleft puts in that spotlight

complement

what follows be and completes it: What I like is the noise

One more: a relative pronoun — who, which, that — stands in for a noun and opens a clause about it. When it is the subject of that clause, nothing else may be.

Language focus · Reported speech

Backshift: one step back

After said, told, announced — the reported tense steps back

present simple — “I love that car”→past simple — he loved that car
past simple — “I drove it”→past perfect — he had driven it
present perfect — “I have driven it”→past perfect — he had driven it
“I will test it”→he would test it
“I can test it”→he could test it
“I must test it”→he had to test it

Two tenses can land on the same one: a past simple and a present perfect both come out as the past perfect, so the report loses a distinction the original had.

Language focus · Reported speech

When the tenses stay where they are

Still true now

Clarkson said that he loves fast cars. The liking has not stopped, so the present may stay.

Reported straight away

She has just said that the shoot starts at six. No time has passed, so nothing needs to move.

Already past

would, could, should, might, ought to, had to have nowhere further back to go.

Both versions are correct English. He said he loves fast cars and he said he loved fast cars are each right — the shifted one simply holds the statement further away from you.

Language focus · Reported speech

The other things that shift

People and things

I → he/she · we → they · my → his/her · this car → that car · these → those

Time and place

now → then · today → that day · tomorrow → the next day · yesterday → the day before · here → there

Shift them only when the situation has actually changed. Repeating tomorrow an hour later in the same room is right; repeating it a week later in another city is not.

Language focus · Conditionals

The third conditional

The form

If + past perfect  →  would have + past participle

The meaning

A past that did not happen, and the past result it would have had. If the crew had checked the forecast, they would not have lost a day.

Both halves are past and both are unreal: the crew did not check, and they did lose the day. Might have and could have replace would have when the result is less certain.

Language focus · Conditionals

The mixed conditional

Third — past result

If Hammond had not crashed in 2006, the crew would not have rebuilt the dragster.

Mixed — present result

If Hammond had not crashed in 2006, the show would not have such strict safety rules today.

The if-clause is identical in both. Only the result moves: would have + past participle looks back, would + infinitive looks at now. today, now and still are the usual signals.

Language focus · Conditionals

Never in the if-clause

Not would

If Clarkson would have checked…  →  If Clarkson had checked…

Not will

If it will rain tomorrow…  →  If it rains tomorrow…

The condition sits one step further back than you expect, and will and would belong in the result clause only. The one exception is a polite request — If you would wait here a moment — which is not really a condition at all.

Language focus · Cleft sentences

It-clefts

The frame

It + be + focus + that/who + the rest of the sentence.

The focus can be almost anything

a person: It was the Stig who set the lap.
an object: It was the Veyron that he called the greatest.
a time: It was in 2006 that Hammond crashed.

Subjects, objects, times and places all take the spotlight equally well, so “an it-cleft emphasises the subject” is not the rule. Use who for a person and that for anything else — that is acceptable for a person too.

Language focus · Cleft sentences

Wh-clefts, or pseudo-clefts

The frame

What + clause + be + focus. What I like is the arguing.

Against the it-cleft

The it-cleft names the focus first; the wh-cleft makes you wait for it. It is the arguing that I like / What I like is the arguing.

The focus of a wh-cleft is the complement of be, so it can be a noun phrase or a whole clause: What the three of them do best is argue about cars.

Language focus · Cleft sentences

that and which — never what

After an it-cleft

It was the Veyron that he called the greatest car ever made. Never the Veyron what he called.

Why the mistake happens

What belongs at the front of a wh-cleft, where it means the thing that. Inside an it-cleft that job has already been done by It.

A related trap: which as the subject of its own clause needs no pronoun after it. …genuinely enthusiastic about cars, which is rare on television.

Language focus · Complex passives

It is thought that…

The frame

It + is/was + said, thought, believed, reported, known + that + a full clause.

What it does

It reports an opinion without saying whose. It is thought that the show changed motoring television.

This pattern is correct English and often the easier one to write: it keeps the clause intact, so no tense has to be folded into an infinitive.

Language focus · Complex passives

The subject moves to the front

Same time as the report

The show is said to be the most watched of its kind. Subject + passive reporting verb + to + infinitive.

Earlier than the report

The show is said to have changed motoring television. The perfect infinitive puts the event before the report.

The two patterns say the same thing. What you cannot do is mix them: The show is thought that it changed… takes the subject from one and the clause from the other.

Language focus · Complex passives

Verbs that take no continuous

A state, not an activity

know, believe, understand, mean, own, seem, prefer, need, belong

So the report stays simple

James May is known to… — never is being known.

A few of them have a second, active meaning that does take the continuous — I am seeing the producer at four, he is being difficult — but never in the stative sense.

🗣Discussion · in pairs

A presenter has walked off a shoot in the middle of a film. You are the production team, and the press are already calling.

  • One of you took the phone call. Report what the presenter actually said — three sentences, and not one word quoted directly.
  • Swap. Argue about what should have been done differently that morning, in the third conditional throughout. A bare past simple loses the argument.
  • Both: agree one sentence for the press that names nobody. Start it It is thought that… or The decision is said to…

Write the statement the production company releases the next morning: what was said, what was decided, what happens now. Report everything — no direct quotation anywhere — and use one cleft to put the emphasis where you want it.
