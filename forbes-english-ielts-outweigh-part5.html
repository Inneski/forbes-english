Weighing It Up

Two more essay types, two more band 9 answers taken apart — and the two ways a well-written essay still fails to answer the question

C1 · IELTS Writing Task 2 Outweigh & Problem–Solution Band 9 vs band 6
Two types, two traps

Read the instruction, not the topic

"Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?" demands a verdict. Listing both sides and stopping is the single most common way this type is failed.

"What problems… and what measures?" demands two matched halves. Every measure must answer a problem you actually raised.

Both band 6 answers in this lesson are competently written. Each one fails on the instruction above, and nothing else in them can make up for it.

Essay one · Outweigh

The question asks you to decide

Many cities are restricting or banning private cars from their central areas. Do the advantages of this development outweigh the disadvantages?

"Outweigh" is a verb of comparison. An essay that presents two lists of equal weight has described a set of scales without reading them.

You may decide either way, or say the balance is close. What you cannot do is leave it to the reader to work out what you think.

Band 9 · paragraph 1

The verdict, in the second sentence

A growing number of city authorities have closed their central districts to private traffic, either partially or altogether, usually in the name of cleaner air. Although the policy imposes real costs on some residents, I believe its advantages clearly outweigh them.

"Although…" concedes before it claims. One clause admits the other side exists, which stops the verdict sounding naive.

"Clearly outweigh" answers the question in the words it was asked in. No examiner has to interpret anything.

Band 9 · paragraph 2

Two gains, one of them slow

The clearest gain is the one the policy was designed for: air that is measurably cleaner. Roadside pollution falls quickly when traffic is removed, and the benefit lands hardest on the people who live on the busiest streets, who are rarely the wealthiest. A second gain is slower but larger. Street space released from parking becomes room for pedestrians, cycle lanes and trees, and cities that have made the change report more people walking into shops rather than fewer.

Two advantages, ranked and dated. One is immediate and one accrues over years — saying so is what turns a list into an argument. The last clause quietly kills the strongest objection shopkeepers make, without naming it yet.

Question 1

Ranked, not listed

The writer calls one advantage "clearest" and the other "slower but larger". What does that ranking buy?

Band 9 · paragraph 3

The objection, at full strength

The costs fall unevenly, and that is the strongest objection. A delivery driver, a plumber carrying tools, or a carer visiting several elderly clients in a morning cannot simply switch to a bus, and a scheme designed only around commuters will punish exactly the workers a city depends on. This is an argument for exemptions and a serious freight plan, however, rather than against the restriction itself: permits for trades and carers, and deliveries outside the busiest hours.

Give the objection its best version, then answer it. The writer names three real people it would hurt — and then shows the objection argues for a better scheme rather than no scheme. A disadvantage you have disarmed weighs less; that is how the scales actually tip.

Band 9 · paragraph 4

Say why the balance falls that way

Weighed against each other, the advantages are broad and permanent while the disadvantages are concentrated and largely fixable. Cleaner air and better streets reach everyone who lives in or visits the centre, whereas the costs land on particular groups whose needs can be planned for. On that basis the balance falls in favour of restriction, provided the exemptions are written before the scheme starts rather than after the complaints arrive.

Broad and permanent against concentrated and fixable — four words that state the criterion the verdict was reached on.

The condition survives to the last line, so the essay ends as one argument rather than two halves and a summary.

Band 6 · same question

Everything except an answer

The first advantage is pollution. Cars make a lot of pollution and this is very bad for the environment and also for people's health. If there are no cars, the air will be cleaner and people will be more healthy. […]

In conclusion, banning cars in city centres has many advantages and also many disadvantages. Both sides have good arguments. Governments should think carefully and make the best decision for everybody.

It never decides. "Both sides have good arguments" is the sentence the question was written to prevent. Note also "more healthy" for healthier, and a conclusion that hands the decision to governments, when the question asked the writer to make it.

Question 2

What would raise it most?

That answer has four paragraphs, clear organisation and reasonable range. One change would lift it further than any other. Which?

Essay two · Problem & measure

Two halves that have to match

In many countries, people are living longer, and the number of adults over seventy is rising rapidly. What problems does this cause, and what measures could be taken to address them?

Two problems, well chosen, beat four listed. Each one needs enough space to show why it is a problem, not merely that it is one.

Every measure must answer a problem you raised. A solution to something you never mentioned reads as a second essay glued on.

Band 9 · paragraph 1

Name both halves before you start

Populations across the developed world are ageing faster than the pension and care systems built to support them. The difficulties this creates are principally financial and practical, and the most promising responses involve changing what old age is expected to look like rather than simply spending more on it.

"Financial and practical" tells the reader there are exactly two problems coming, and which order they arrive in.

The measures get a direction, not a preview. One clause sets the principle the solutions will share.

Band 9 · paragraph 2

The problems, one costed, one not

The immediate problem is arithmetic. A pension system funded by those in work depends on a ratio between workers and retirees, and that ratio is deteriorating in almost every wealthy country as fewer young people enter the workforce. The second problem is harder to price: a growing number of people live for years in a state where they can manage at home only with help, and the relatives who have historically supplied that help are now in full-time employment themselves, often a long way from their parents.

Each problem is explained by its mechanism, not asserted. Not "pensions are expensive" but the ratio that makes them so; not "people are lonely" but the specific labour that used to be free and no longer is. A problem the reader can see working is a problem a measure can be aimed at.

Question 3

Why only two problems?

The writer could easily have named five problems. Why is stopping at two the stronger choice here?

Band 9 · paragraph 3

Measures aimed at those problems

The most effective measure is also the least popular: to uncouple the retirement age from a figure fixed when life expectancy was fifteen years shorter, and let it rise as people live longer. Alongside that, countries which have invested in home adaptation and community nursing rather than residential places have kept people independent for longer at lower cost, and have taken pressure off relatives who would otherwise cut their hours to provide care — which addresses the second problem and part of the first at once.

Measure one answers problem one; measure two answers both. The final clause says so out loud, which is the cheapest way to prove the halves are matched. Note also that the writer admits the first measure is unpopular — a measure presented as costless is one nobody has thought about.

Band 9 · paragraph 4

A conclusion with a thought in it

Ageing populations are not a crisis so much as the predictable result of success. The measures that work treat later life as a stage to be planned for and resourced, not as an emergency to be absorbed at the last minute.

Two sentences are enough. Nothing new is argued; the frame the essay used is simply named.

Compare it with "if we all work together we can solve it" — the same length, and no content whatever.

Band 6 · same question

Solutions to other people's problems

The main problem is money. Old people need pensions and the government must pay a lot of money for this. […] Firstly, the government should spend more money on hospitals and build more of them. Secondly, young people should respect old people and visit their grandparents more often. Moreover, people should do exercise and eat healthy food when they are young so they will not be sick when they are old.

The measures do not answer the problems. The problem was that pensions cost more than the state can raise; the first measure is to spend more, the second is a matter of manners, and the third solves a problem the essay never raised.

Question 4

"The government should spend more"

Why is "the government should spend more money on hospitals" such a weak measure in this particular essay?
