IELTS Speaking Part 3

The discussion: answering for people in general, and holding the line when the examiner pushes back

C1 · Advanced Speaking Part 3 13 questions
Before you start

Part 1 asked about you. Part 3 asks about people.

The person changes

Part 1: “Do you enjoy cooking?” Part 3: “Why do fewer people cook at home now?” The second question is not about your kitchen. Answer for the group.

If the answer would still stand with you removed from it, it is a Part 3 answer.

Three phrases that generalise

Tend to, on the whole, by and large. Each says “this is what is usually true”, and each leaves room for the exceptions the examiner is about to raise.

Tend to takes a plain verb: people tend to arrive late, never tend to arriving late.

Your own life still counts

One example, placed after the general claim, shows you mean it. A case in point is… marks it as an illustration rather than as the whole answer.

One example is support. Three examples is a Part 1 answer that has run long.

Before you start

If the examiner pushes back, that is the task, not a verdict

Pushback is part of the test

Part 3 examiners often push back — asking whether a view holds everywhere, or putting the other side — however good the answer. It is not a signal that the answer was weak: it is how Part 3 finds out what you can do under pressure.

A well-made point can attract more pushback, not less, because it is worth pushing.

Concede, then qualify

Give the other side something true, then keep your ground: That is true up to a point, though… or I would accept that for smaller towns, but…

The clause after the concession carries your position. Stopping at the concession is how a point gets lost.

Changing your mind is allowed

Say it aloud and it reads as thinking: Actually, now that I say it… or On reflection, I would put that differently.

A silent reversal confuses the listener. A signalled one is coherence, and Fluency and Coherence is a quarter of the mark.

Before you start

How sure are you? Say so once, and mean it.

Hedging is precise language

Broadly speaking, arguably, in most cases. These are precise words doing a precise job, which Lexical Resource rewards — and modal verbs and frames like it is argued that show grammatical range as well.

A hedge is not a filler. Sort of and kind of are fillers — they grade nothing.

Move the claim off yourself

It is often argued that… and There is a case for saying… report a position without signing your name to it, which leaves you free to take it apart a sentence later.

Useful on a topic you know little about: you can run the argument without claiming it.

Hedge once, not everywhere

One graded claim reads as judgement. Four in a row reads as evasion, and an answer carrying a hedge on every clause has stopped saying anything at all.

Commit in the main clause, limit in the clause after it. That is the shape.

    🗣Discussion · in pairs

    One of you examines, one answers, and a phone holds the clock. The examiner asks four questions on a single topic and must disagree at least twice. Five minutes, then swap.

    • Candidate: answer for people in general first, and only then give one example of your own.
    • Examiner: push back on the strongest thing you hear, not the weakest. Ask whether it holds everywhere.
    • Candidate: concede what is fair, keep the rest, and say how sure you are without hedging every clause.
