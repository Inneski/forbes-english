Section I

Advanced Question Formation

At C2 level, direct questions are often too blunt for diplomatic or academic contexts. Mastery requires command of embedded structures, rhetorical inversions, and cleft constructions that foreground meaning with precision.

01 · Embedded & Indirect Questions

Embedded questions place a question inside a statement or another question. Critically, they use statement word order — no subject-verb inversion — after an introductory clause.

The question is whether [S + V + …] — NOT whether [V + S + …]
Direct
Why did the Security Council fail to act on the crisis?
Embedded
The question that must be addressed is why the Security Council failed to act — and whose strategic interests that inaction served.
Rhetorical
The real issue is whether multilateralism can survive the unilateralist pressures within the permanent membership.

02 · Negative & Adversarial Questions

Negative questions challenge assumptions and are central to political debate. They imply accusation, irony, or disbelief. Word order shifts depending on whether the negative is contracted.

Isn't it the case that… / Can it not be argued that… / Was it not precisely…
Challenge
Isn't it precisely this kind of economic coercion that undermines the rules-based international order?
Formal uncontracted
Can it not be argued that the sanctions regime has had an asymmetrically punitive effect on civilian populations?

03 · Tag Questions at C2

Beyond simple confirmation, tag questions at mastery level signal nuance, sarcasm, or appeals to shared knowledge in policy debate.

Positive statement + negative tag / Negative statement + positive tag
Seeking consensus
The expansion of NATO was always going to provoke a response from Moscow, wasn't it?
Ironic / challenging
Sovereignty doesn't really apply when it's inconvenient for great powers, does it?
Hypothetical tag
We can't simply redraw borders without destabilising an entire region, can we?
Emphatic positive
This treaty did, in fact, set a precedent for humanitarian intervention, didn't it?

04 · Cleft Questions for Emphasis

Cleft and pseudo-cleft structures foreground specific elements — essential in geopolitical argument where emphasis shapes interpretation.

It is/was [X] that/who… / What [S+V] is/was [Y]
It-cleft
It is the unresolved question of self-determination that continues to fuel the conflict in the region.
Wh-cleft
What the international community failed to anticipate was the speed of the geopolitical realignment following the withdrawal.
Section II

If-Clauses & Conditional Logic

Conditionals are tools for strategic reasoning, not just grammar. Mastering them means distinguishing what is possible, probable, and contrary to fact — and signalling those distinctions precisely.

C2 Insight: The most sophisticated conditional use in diplomatic and academic English combines types (mixed conditionals), employs formal inversions, and deploys subordinators like unless, provided that, and on the condition that to add precision.
Type 1
Real / Open
if + present → will/can/may
Type 2
Hypothetical
if + past simple → would/could/might
Type 3
Counterfactual
if + past perfect → would have + pp
Mixed
Cross-temporal
past ↔ present consequence

Type 1 · Real / Open Conditions

Used when the condition is genuinely possible. In geopolitics, this is the language of policy statements, negotiations, and operative clauses in treaties.

If + present simple/continuous, will / can / may + base verb
If the ceasefire holds through the winter, there will be a window for sustained diplomatic engagement.
If the sanctions are maintained without humanitarian exemptions, civilian suffering may intensify beyond tolerable thresholds.

Type 2 · Hypothetical / Policy Modelling

Imagines a different present or future reality. Critical for think-tank analysis, scenario planning, and political speeches advocating alternative policies.

If + past simple, would / could / might + base verb
If the permanent members were elected rather than appointed, the Security Council would arguably be more representative — though potentially less decisive.
If China were to significantly reduce its dependency on dollar-denominated trade, the architecture of global finance would shift irrevocably.

Type 3 · Counterfactual / Historical Analysis

Reflects on past events — a cornerstone of diplomatic history, accountability discourse, and scholarly retrospection.

If + past perfect, would / could / might + have + past participle
If the League of Nations had possessed meaningful enforcement mechanisms, it might have been able to contain the expansionist ambitions of the Axis powers.
If the Minsk Agreements had been implemented in good faith by all parties, the 2022 escalation could, at least theoretically, have been averted.

Mixed · Cross-temporal Conditionals

Mix time frames to link past causes to present consequences, or current realities to hypothetical pasts. A hallmark of C2 analytical writing.

If + past perfect (past) → would + base verb (present consequence)
Past → Present
If the post-Cold War order had been built on more equitable foundations, we would not be witnessing the current fragmentation of multilateral institutions.
If + past simple (present) → would + have + pp (past consequence)
Present → Past
If the EU were a genuinely federal state, it would have responded to the 2008 financial crisis with far greater coherence.

Advanced · Inversion & Alternatives to "if"

Replacing "if" with formal inversions or subordinators marks high-register academic and diplomatic English. These structures are common in official documents, treaties, and scholarly papers.

Were [S] to [V]… / Had [S] [past participle]… / Should [S] [V]…
Were … to (Type 2)
Were the bloc to impose a full trade embargo, the domestic political consequences for member states would be severe.
Had … (Type 3)
Had the administration heeded early intelligence warnings, a more measured escalation strategy might have prevailed.
Should (Type 1, formal)
Should the negotiations collapse, the parties have agreed to return to the framework established under the 2019 accord.
Unless / provided that
Diplomatic normalisation is inconceivable unless both parties agree to meaningful confidence-building measures.
Section III

Geopolitical Vocabulary

Familiarity with the precise meaning and collocations of these terms is expected at C2 level. Hover over cards to explore.

Power & Diplomacy

Geopolitical realignment
A fundamental shift in alliances or power relations between states, often following a crisis or war.
Hegemony
The dominance of one state over others, exercised politically, economically, or militarily.
Multilateralism
Structured cooperation among three or more states on matters of policy or security.
Sovereignty
The supreme authority of a state over its territory and population, free from external interference.
Sphere of influence
A region where a state informally projects its power and expects deference from others.
Deterrence
Discouraging an adversary's action through the credible threat of disproportionate retaliation.

Conflict & Resolution

Proxy conflict
A war fought by surrogate forces on behalf of larger powers who avoid direct confrontation.
Sanctions regime
A coordinated system of economic and political penalties imposed upon a state or actor.
Escalation dominance
The capacity to match or outpace any move by an adversary at every rung of the escalation ladder.
Brinkmanship
Deliberately pushing a dangerous situation to the edge of conflict to extract concessions.
Humanitarian corridor
A negotiated safe passage for civilian evacuation or aid delivery during active conflict.
Compellence
The use of threats or force to actively change an adversary's behaviour — the inverse of deterrence.

Institutions & Norms

Veto power
The right of the five permanent UNSC members to block any substantive resolution unilaterally.
R2P doctrine
The Responsibility to Protect: a norm obligating the international community to intervene against mass atrocities.
Rules-based order
The system of international norms, laws, and institutions built after 1945 to manage state behaviour.
Non-alignment
A foreign policy stance of refusing to join military blocs; associated with the Global South.
Extraterritoriality
The extension of one state's jurisdiction beyond its own borders, often via secondary sanctions.
Democratic backsliding
The gradual erosion of democratic institutions, norms, and practices within a state.

Exercises

Work through each exercise before revealing the model answer. Focus on register, precision, and grammatical accuracy in geopolitical contexts.

Exercise 01
Rewrite as Embedded Questions
Questions

Rewrite the following direct questions as embedded questions suitable for a diplomatic communiqué or academic paper. Remember: no inversion after the introductory clause.

a) Why did the Security Council fail to act on the humanitarian crisis?

b) Will the ceasefire agreement hold beyond the initial 72-hour window?

c) Who bears ultimate responsibility for the breakdown of the peace talks?

a) The question that must be addressed is why the Security Council failed to act on the humanitarian crisis — and whose strategic interests that inaction served.

b) Observers remain profoundly uncertain as to whether the ceasefire agreement will hold beyond the initial 72-hour window, given the deep mistrust on both sides.

c) It remains deeply contested as to who bears ultimate responsibility for the collapse of the peace process — a question that will inevitably shape the terms of any subsequent tribunal.
Exercise 02
Identify & Analyse the Conditional
If-Clauses

For each sentence, identify the conditional type (1, 2, 3, or mixed) and explain what meaning it conveys in geopolitical terms.

"Had the international community intervened earlier, the civilian death toll would, in all likelihood, have been significantly lower."

"Were the alliance to invoke Article 5, member states would be obligated to respond — irrespective of domestic political pressures."

Sentence 1: Type 3 (counterfactual). It analyses a past failure — the non-intervention — and its consequences. "In all likelihood" adds epistemic caution appropriate for scholarly analysis. The speaker assigns moral responsibility without direct accusation, a typical move in accountability discourse.

Sentence 2: Type 2 using formal inversion (Were … to). It models a hypothetical but plausible future scenario. "Obligated" signals legal/treaty register. The clause "irrespective of domestic political pressures" acknowledges the real tension between treaty obligations and electoral politics.
Exercise 03
Complete with the Correct Conditional
If-Clauses

Fill in the blanks using the most appropriate conditional structure. Consider which type best fits the geopolitical context.

a) If the post-Soviet transition ____ (manage) more carefully by Western institutions, Russia ____ (may / integrate) far more deeply into the European security architecture.

b) Should Beijing ____ (decide) to enforce a naval blockade of Taiwan, the consequences ____ (be) felt across the global semiconductor supply chain within days.

c) If global temperatures ____ (exceed) 2°C above pre-industrial levels, entire island nations ____ (face) existential territorial loss within decades.

a) If the post-Soviet transition had been managed more carefully by Western institutions, Russia might have integrated far more deeply into the European security architecture — though structural and historical factors would still have posed significant obstacles. [Type 3]

b) Should Beijing decide to enforce a naval blockade of Taiwan, the consequences would be felt across the global semiconductor supply chain within days, triggering an unprecedented diplomatic and economic crisis. [Type 1, formal inversion]

c) If global temperatures exceed 2°C above pre-industrial levels, entire island nations will face existential territorial loss within decades — a scenario that renders conventional notions of sovereignty legally and practically untenable. [Type 1]
Exercise 04
Cleft Questions & Emphasis
Questions Advanced

Rewrite each sentence as an it-cleft or wh-cleft to foreground the underlined element. Then write a follow-up rhetorical question using a tag question or negative question.

The failure to implement the arms embargo allowed the conflict to escalate.

Washington's withdrawal from the Paris Agreement signalled a new era of unilateralism.

Sentence 1 — It-cleft: It was the failure to implement the arms embargo that allowed the conflict to escalate to the scale we witnessed.
Rhetorical follow-up: Can it not be argued that this failure was entirely foreseeable — and that the relevant actors chose wilful blindness over accountability?

Sentence 2 — Wh-cleft: What signalled a new era of unilateralism most unambiguously was Washington's withdrawal from the Paris Agreement and the diplomatic vacuum it created.
Rhetorical follow-up: The United States was always going to be a reluctant multilateral partner on climate, wasn't it?
Exercise 05
Mixed Conditionals in Analysis
If-Clauses Advanced

Write two original sentences using the geopolitical context of your choice:

1. A mixed conditional linking a past condition to a present consequence.

2. A mixed conditional linking a present reality to a hypothetical past outcome.

Past → Present: If the Bretton Woods institutions had been designed with the Global South's structural vulnerabilities in mind, we would not be witnessing the current wave of debt-driven instability across Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.

Present → Past: If the European Union were a genuinely unified foreign policy actor rather than a coalition of competing national interests, it would have exerted far greater leverage during the initial phases of the Ukraine crisis.

Discussion Prompts

Use these prompts in pairs or groups. Responses should demonstrate the grammar studied throughout this lesson. Aim for register consistency and analytical depth.

§
A
The Limits of Sovereignty

The R2P doctrine has been invoked selectively and controversially. If sovereignty is genuinely inviolable, how can humanitarian intervention ever be legitimate? And if it is not, who decides when the threshold has been crossed — and on what grounds?

Target grammar: embedded questions ("the real issue is whether…") · type 2 conditionals ("were the UN to…") · adversarial tag questions
B
The Architecture of Deterrence

Some analysts argue nuclear deterrence has prevented great-power conflict since 1945. Had the Cold War been fought between non-nuclear states, would the outcome have been more or less destructive? What does this imply for non-proliferation policy today?

Target grammar: type 3 conditionals · mixed conditionals · cleft structures ("it is the logic of deterrence itself that…")
C
Economic Interdependence as Peace

Liberal theory assumes trade creates dependency that disincentivises conflict. Yet Russia and Europe were deeply economically integrated before 2022. Can interdependence be a reliable deterrent? If not, which assumptions of the liberal order require fundamental revision?

Target grammar: negative questions ("isn't it the case that…") · type 1 and 2 conditionals · "provided that / unless" structures
D
The Global South & Multipolarity

Argue for or against: "Were a genuinely multipolar world order to emerge, it would not necessarily be a more just or stable one — merely a differently hierarchical one." What assumptions underlie this claim, and to what extent are they warranted?

Target grammar: formal inversion conditionals · embedded counter-arguments ("what proponents of multipolarity overlook is…") · ironic tag questions
E
Counterfactual History

Choose one juncture and construct a detailed counterfactual argument using mixed and type 3 conditionals: ① US non-entry into WWI · ② The League of Nations including the US · ③ The Cuban Missile Crisis escalating · ④ The 2003 Iraq War not occurring.

Target grammar: mixed conditionals · type 3 · formal inversions (had… / were… to) · rhetorical embedded questions
