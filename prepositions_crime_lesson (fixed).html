What is a preposition?

A preposition is a small but powerful word that connects a noun or pronoun to the rest of the sentence. It tells you where, when, how, or in what direction something happens.

In crime fiction, prepositions place the detective at the scene, the suspect around the corner, and the critical clue beneath the floorboards. Without them, the story falls apart.

Key Rule

The preposition phrase

A preposition is always followed by a noun or pronoun — together they form a prepositional phrase. This phrase acts like an adverb (telling when, where, how) or an adjective (describing a noun).

The detective found the clue [under the floorboards] — prepositional phrase acting as adverb (where).
The man [in the trench coat] was the prime suspect — prepositional phrase acting as adjective (which man).

Prepositions in action

Study these sentences from the case files. The prepositions are highlighted — notice how they anchor each action to a place, time, or manner.

Place prepositions — where the crime happens

A masked figure was waiting behind the door at the end of the corridor.
The ransom note was left on the table, between two flickering candlesticks.

Time prepositions — when it happened
The heist was planned during the summer and executed on a stormy Friday night.
The suspect disappeared at midnight, just before the guards changed shift.
After three years in prison, he was finally ready for revenge.
She had been watching the target since January, waiting until the moment was right.
Direction prepositions — chases & escapes
The hitman moved through the crowd and disappeared into the alley.
She ran across the rooftop and leaped toward the fire escape.
The getaway car sped along the waterfront and turned onto the bridge.
Manner prepositions — how it was done
He broke into the vault with a stolen key card, acting without hesitation.
Despite acting against orders, she solved the case by working alone.
Without a single alibi, the suspect was charged with first-degree murder.

Closing Statement

Now give your own account

You have placed the suspect, the weapon and the hour. The last thing any investigator has to do is say it out loud — and then write it down so it holds up. Prepositions are what make a statement precise enough to be believed.

Speaking · in pairs
  • One of you is the detective, the other the last person to see the victim. Ask five questions that each begin with a preposition of place or time: At what time…? In which room…? Since when…?
  • Describe this room to your partner with their eyes closed, precisely enough for them to draw it. Use at least six prepositions of place.
  • Give your alibi for last Tuesday evening in one unbroken minute. Your partner listens for a preposition used wrongly — one slip and you are a suspect.
  • Tell the story of a crime you have seen in a film. Move it through time: before, during, after, by the time.

Writing · 180–220 words

Write the detective’s report on the case. Say where each person was, when they were there, and how the evidence moved from one place to another. Every sentence should earn its prepositions — a report that says the knife was found tells us nothing; the knife was found beneath the floorboards, behind the stairs, three days after the body closes a case.
